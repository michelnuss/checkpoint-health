# US Physician Licensure and Telehealth Law for Checkpoint (as of Nov 7, 2026)

Scope note: research was limited (~16 tool calls). Statute text was read through a fetch tool that summarizes pages, so "statute" items below are summaries of the official statute pages, not verbatim quotes. Verify exact wording with Florida counsel before relying. This is research, not legal advice.

## 1. Which state's law governs; physician-patient relationship via asynchronous / AI-prepared intake

### Takeaway
Across the states checked, the governing license requirement attaches to where the patient is located, not where the physician sits. Florida says so explicitly (venue and registration), California and Illinois guidance say a license is needed for patients located in-state. Store-and-forward is recognized by statute in several states (FL, CA, NY), but some states say a relationship cannot be formed by email/text alone, which matters for a text-review model. No source found addresses AI-prepared intake specifically.

### Cited Findings
- Florida: telehealth care is deemed to occur where the patient is located or in the patient's county of residence; suit may be brought in the patient's county or Leon County — [Fla. Stat. 456.47(5)](http://www.leg.state.fl.us/statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&URL=0400-0499/0456/Sections/0456.47.html) (statute page, summarized)
- Florida defines telehealth as using "synchronous or asynchronous" telecommunications technology, and excludes e-mail messages and facsimile transmissions — [Fla. Stat. 456.47(1)](http://www.leg.state.fl.us/statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&URL=0400-0499/0456/Sections/0456.47.html) (statute)
- Florida: a telehealth provider may evaluate via telehealth; if the evaluation is sufficient to diagnose and treat, no prior medical-history review or physical exam is required first — [Fla. Stat. 456.47(2)(b)](http://www.leg.state.fl.us/statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&URL=0400-0499/0456/Sections/0456.47.html) (statute)
- California: "asynchronous store and forward" is defined as transmission of a patient's medical information from an originating site to a provider at a distant site — [Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code 2290.5(a)(1)](https://law.justia.com/codes/california/2020/code-bpc/division-2/chapter-5/article-12/section-2290-5/) (2020 version via Justia; may have been amended since)
- California: before telehealth, the initiating provider must tell the patient telehealth will be used and obtain verbal or written consent, documented in the record; failure to follow the section is unprofessional conduct; licensing-law standards of practice apply to telehealth — [Cal. B&P 2290.5(b),(d),(g)](https://law.justia.com/codes/california/2020/code-bpc/division-2/chapter-5/article-12/section-2290-5/) (2020 version)
- New York: telehealth includes telemedicine, store and forward, remote patient monitoring, and audio-only; fax and electronic messaging alone are excluded; store and forward is asynchronous transmission of patient-specific information such as digital images or pre-recorded video — [N.Y. Pub. Health Law 2999-cc](https://newyork.public.law/laws/n.y._public_health_law_section_2999-cc) (statute mirror site)
- Washington (committee-amended 2024 bill report, not confirmed enacted in the version read): a relationship may be established through telehealth but not through email, instant messaging, text messaging, or fax alone; out-of-state practitioners need a WA license or may only give limited services (consult with a WA practitioner who stays responsible, specialty assessment/recommendation, or continuity follow-up) — [WA House Bill Report, SB 5481](https://lawfilesext.leg.wa.gov/biennium/2023-24/Htm/Bill%20Reports/House/5481-S.E%20HBR%20HCW%2024.htm)
- CCHP Fall 2025: 38 states, DC and PR allow some exception to the in-state license rule (often consultation with an in-state provider who keeps ultimate responsibility); 18 states plus territories offer telehealth-specific licenses or registrations; some exceptions are narrowing — [CCHP Fall 2025 Executive Summary](https://www.cchpca.org/2025/10/2025_ExecutiveSummary_final.pdf)
- Illinois (commentary): treating a patient physically in Illinois generally requires an Illinois license; the source says a legitimate clinical relationship is needed before diagnosing/prescribing and typically describes real-time audio-video evaluation — [AccountableHQ Illinois 2026 guide](https://www.accountablehq.com/post/illinois-telehealth-regulations-2026-guide-to-licensing-coverage-and-prescribing) (low-authority commentary; did not cite statute)
- Texas: per CCHP, prescriptions must rest on a physician-patient relationship — [CCHP Fall 2025](https://www.cchpca.org/2025/10/2025_ExecutiveSummary_final.pdf). A 2017 law-firm note says Texas SB 1107 removed the in-person-visit precondition and the TMB rules dropped the "face-to-face" requirement — [Sheppard Mullin, Dec 2017](https://www.sheppard.com/insights/blogs/texas-telemedicine-saga-finally-over-the-texas-medical-board-substantially-revises-telemedicine-regulations) (old, commentary)

### Inferences
- Checkpoint should treat the patient's location at time of service as the controlling jurisdiction and need a physician licensed (or exempt/registered) in that state for every state it serves.
- Because FL, NY and WA exclude plain email/text/fax from "telehealth" or relationship formation, a purely text-based exchange could fall outside statutory telehealth definitions; the structured, platform-based case packet and a documented physician review likely help, but this needs state-by-state counsel review.
- The "consultation with a licensed in-state practitioner who retains ultimate responsibility" exceptions probably do not fit Checkpoint, because the patient (not a local clinician) is the originating party.
- AI-prepared intake: no statute or board rule found that specifically governs it; physician remains responsible for the standard of care (FL 456.47(2)(a)).

### Gaps
- No primary-source text found on AI-assisted intake or on state "AI in health care" disclosure laws (several states enacted such laws in 2025-26; not researched).
- Texas current statute (Occ. Code ch. 111, 22 TAC ch. 174) and California current 2290.5/2052 text not read directly; Illinois Telehealth Act and NY licensure/out-of-state rules not read from primary sources.
- Whether WA SB 5481 was enacted and in what form was not confirmed.
- Which states require a prior in-person exam or are restrictive on async care: not systematically verified (CCHP Policy Finder would answer; not accessed).

## 2. Florida in depth (Fla. Stat. 456.47)

### Takeaway
Florida is permissive: asynchronous telehealth is expressly allowed, no prior exam is required if the remote evaluation suffices, in-person standard of care applies, and out-of-state physicians may serve Florida patients only through a registration (or compact license, or an exemption). Unregistered practice by an out-of-state physician is the main risk.

### Cited Findings
- "Telehealth provider" includes Florida-licensed practitioners, practitioners licensed under a multistate compact Florida belongs to, and out-of-state providers registered under subsection (4) — [Fla. Stat. 456.47(1)](http://www.leg.state.fl.us/statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&URL=0400-0499/0456/Sections/0456.47.html)
- Standard of care: must act within scope and meet the professional standard of care that applies to in-person care in Florida — 456.47(2)(a), same source
- Prescribing: Schedule II via telehealth only for psychiatric disorders, inpatient hospital treatment, hospice, or nursing home residents; telehealth may not be used to perform an abortion — 456.47(2)(c),(f), same source
- Records: documented to the same standard as in-person care; confidential under ss. 395.3025(4) and 456.057 — 456.47(3), same source
- Out-of-state registration (4): requires active unencumbered out-of-state license, no discipline in prior 5 years, a Florida registered agent, malpractice coverage compliance; report restrictions/discipline within 5 business days; no Florida office or in-person care; department publishes a registrant list and may discipline — same source
- Exemptions (6): no registration needed for care in response to an emergency medical condition, or care provided in consultation with a Florida-licensed professional who has ultimate authority over diagnosis and treatment — same source
- Rulemaking authority (7) to boards/department — same source
- Amendment history on the page: ch. 2019-137, 2020-156, 2022-26, 2023-21, 2023-63, 2023-132 (no 2025-26 session law listed in the page summary) — same source
- Flagged as relevant: 2023 Florida Senate analyses of bills amending telehealth are at flsenate.gov (e.g., [CS/HB 267 analysis](https://flsenate.gov/Session/Bill/2023/267/Analyses/h0267e.HHS.PDF)); content not read.

### Inferences
- Florida-licensed physicians employed by a Florida professional entity fit the cleanest path: no registration, same standards as in-person care.
- Patient identity/location verification: the statute summary I read contains no explicit identity-verification mandate; it is likely a board-rule or prudential best practice (Gap).
- Florida also has no statutory asynchronous restriction; text-only exchanges may fall outside "telehealth" per the e-mail/fax exclusion, so use a secure platform rather than email.

### Gaps
- Exact statutory text (only a summary read); Florida Board of Medicine rules (Rule 64B8) on telehealth, informed consent, and identity verification not found.
- Whether 2025 or 2026 Florida sessions amended 456.47 was not confirmed.
- Florida informed-consent statute (766.103) interplay not researched.

## 3. Interstate Medical Licensure Compact (IMLC)

### Takeaway
IMLC expedites, but does not replace, state licensure: a physician still gets a separate license in each state. Member count is cited inconsistently (42 in Oct 2025 per CCHP; 44 plus DC and Guam in 2026 per AMA). Florida joined in 2024. It helps a physician network cover many states, though eligibility is strict and NY and CA are historically not members (not verified here).

### Cited Findings
- 44 states plus DC and Guam participate; over 200,000 licenses issued; about 3,633 in March 2026 alone — [AMA](https://www.ama-assn.org/practice-management/digital-health/cross-state-licensing-process-now-live-30-states) (page URL says "30 states" but content updated 2026); contradicted in count by [CCHP Fall 2025](https://www.cchpca.org/2025/10/2025_ExecutiveSummary_final.pdf) (42 states, DC, Guam as of Oct 2025; plausibly just older)
- Eligibility: full unrestricted license in a member state of principal license (SPL) with residence/25% practice/employment tie; accredited med school and residency; passed USMLE/COMLEX within three attempts; current ABMS/AOA board certification; no discipline, criminal, controlled-substance, or pending-investigation history — AMA, same source
- Cost: $700 application fee, plus additional fees per state — AMA, same source (state fee amounts not found)
- Process: apply via IMLCC, SPL reviews and runs background check, issues Letter of Qualification (about 38 days on average, 55% under a month); then choose states; Letter valid one year — AMA, same source
- Florida: passed SB 7016 in the 2024 session, becoming the 40th state, onboarded with IMLC Commission in fall 2024 — [Florida Board of Medicine](https://flboardofmedicine.gov/licensure-compact/)
- CCHP: each compact generally requires an active home-state license plus a separate compact license — CCHP Fall 2025
- Illinois joined as the 10th member (older Hinshaw alert; Illinois is a member) — [Hinshaw](https://www.hinshawlaw.com/en/insights/healthcare-alert/illinois-eases-telehealth-use-by-becoming-10th-member-of-interstate-medical-licensure-compact)
- Florida 456.47 expressly counts compact-licensed practitioners as telehealth providers (see section 2).

### Inferences
- Recruiting physicians who already hold IMLC-eligible credentials lets a network add many states quickly (weeks), but each state license also carries renewal and state fees; the network is capped by physician eligibility.
- IMLC does not cover states outside the compact; non-member states need conventional licensure. Verify membership of CA, NY, TX, WA, IL on imlcc.org before launch (I could not load the IMLCC map).

### Gaps
- imlcc.org pages failed to load content; exact current member list, state-fee amounts, and whether CA/NY/PA etc. have joined unknown.

## 4. Other states: CA, TX, NY, IL, WA

### Takeaway
No source confirmed a state that categorically bans async physician review, but several limit relationship formation or require in-state licensure with narrow exceptions. Detailed per-state analysis is incomplete.

### Cited Findings
- California: patient consent (verbal or written) and documentation required; store and forward defined; licensing standards apply — Cal. B&P 2290.5 (see section 1). The Medical Board's telehealth page did not contain telehealth content ([MBC](https://mbc.ca.gov/Resources/Medical-Resources/telehealth.aspx)).
- New York: statute recognizes store and forward as a telehealth modality; fax/electronic messaging alone excluded — NY PHL 2999-cc (section 1)
- Washington: see section 1 (text/email cannot establish relationship; out-of-state limited services); enactment unconfirmed
- Illinois: in-state license needed for patients located in Illinois (commentary only)
- Texas: prescription needs physician-patient relationship (CCHP); 2017 reforms removed face-to-face precondition (Sheppard Mullin)
- CCHP: Texas HB 1700 directs licensing agencies to standardize telehealth consent rules — CCHP Fall 2025
- Medicaid store-and-forward: 40 state programs reimburse; some states define telehealth as real-time only — CCHP Fall 2025 (reimbursement, not licensure)

### Inferences
- Because Checkpoint is cash-pay/consumer, Medicaid definitions matter less, but real-time-only telehealth definitions in a state could affect whether async review qualifies for telehealth-specific protections.
- Launch in Florida first, then add IMLC-eligible states.

### Gaps
- TX Occupations Code and TMB rule text; CA and NY licensure exceptions; IL Telehealth Act; WA RCW; any state with a prior-in-person-exam rule for non-controlled drugs. Not obtained.

## 5. E-prescribing, DEA, Ryan Haight Act

### Takeaway
Scope can exclude controlled substances. The Ryan Haight Act requires at least one in-person evaluation before telemedicine prescribing of controlled substances (with narrow exceptions); COVID-era flexibilities that waive this were extended a fourth time through Dec 31, 2026, and a final special-registration rule is at OIRA.

### Cited Findings
- Ryan Haight Act requires at least one in-person medical evaluation before controlled substance prescribing, with seven listed exceptions (including a special registration DEA has not implemented as of the article) — [Becker's (2017)](https://www.beckershospitalreview.com/?p=100267) (old article, commentary)
- Fourth Temporary Extension published in the Federal Register Dec 31, 2025 (doc 2025-24123), flexibilities run through Dec 31, 2026; DEA-registered practitioners may remotely prescribe Schedule II-V without prior in-person evaluation, subject to federal and state law — [Hooper Lundy](https://www.hooperlundy.com/dea-issues-fourth-extension-of-telemedicine-flexibilities-for-prescribing-controlled-medications-through-2026/) (law firm commentary; citation inferred from link)
- DEA sent the special-registration final rule to OIRA on Aug 25, 2026; OIRA stakeholder meetings in Sept-Oct 2026 and more scheduled in Nov 2026; text unpublished — [Frier Levitt](https://www.frierlevitt.com/?p=60906) (law firm commentary)
- Jan 2025 proposed rule: three registrations (Telemedicine Prescribing Schedules III-V, Advanced Telemedicine Prescribing Schedules II-V, Telemedicine Platform Registration); would have required registration in each state where patients are treated; >6,400 comments; final may differ — Frier Levitt
- Florida limits Schedule II telehealth prescribing to specific settings — Fla. Stat. 456.47(2)(c)
- Florida also bars using telehealth to perform abortions — 456.47(2)(f)
- California AB 82 (effective Jan 1, 2026) bars reporting testosterone or mifepristone prescriptions to CURES — [MBC site](https://mbc.ca.gov/Resources/Medical-Resources/telehealth.aspx) (tangential)

### Inferences
- Recommend a hard exclusion of all controlled substances (and likely abortion-related care) from scope; this avoids DEA registration, the Dec 31, 2026 cliff, and a possible platform registration.
- A final special-registration rule or another extension is likely around year-end 2026; monitor but exclusion makes Checkpoint largely insulated.

### Gaps
- Federal Register primary documents were not read directly; no confirmation of any action after the early Nov 2026 reports.
- Non-controlled e-prescribing rules (EPCS, state e-prescribing mandates, pharmacy dispensing) not researched.

## 6. Informed consent and emergency escalation

### Takeaway
Consent is widely required but varies; emergency-escalation duties are not codified in the sources I found, so they must be built from standard of care and best practice.

### Cited Findings
- 45 states, DC and PR have some telehealth consent requirement in statute, code, or Medicaid policy — [CCHP Fall 2025](https://www.cchpca.org/2025/10/2025_ExecutiveSummary_final.pdf)
- California requires verbal or written consent documented in the record — Cal. B&P 2290.5(b)
- Commentary on Illinois suggests consent should cover modality limits, risks, and how to reach urgent/emergency care, plus recording of locations — AccountableHQ (low authority)
- Louisiana (behavior analysis) example of consent contents: technical failure, risks, privacy, emergency protocols — CCHP Fall 2025
- Florida: standard of care equals in-person care (456.47(2)(a)), which implies recognizing and escalating emergencies

### Inferences
- Collect consent in the MCP flow before case submission; include async limitations, no emergency use, physician license state, privacy, and a statement that no live exam occurs.
- Build red-flag triage (chest pain, severe breathing difficulty, stroke signs, sepsis, etc.) to display 911/ER instructions immediately and have physicians document escalation.

### Gaps
- No statute found imposing a specific emergency-referral duty for async telehealth; Florida Board rules and informed consent statute (Fla. Stat. 766.103) not examined; HIPAA/redaction/AI-disclosure issues belong to other researchers.

## 7. Penalties for unlicensed practice

### Takeaway
Florida treats unlicensed practice as a felony plus administrative and civil penalties. Other states not researched.

### Cited Findings
- Practicing, attempting, or offering to practice a health care profession without an active valid Florida license is a third-degree felony (min. $1,000 fine and 1 year mandatory incarceration per the page summary); second-degree felony if serious bodily injury results; first-degree misdemeanor for inactive/delinquent license under 12 months — [Fla. Stat. 456.065 (via florida.public.law)](https://florida.public.law/statutes/fla._stat._456.065) (statute mirror; summarized)
- Cease and desist may issue to anyone who aids and abets unlicensed practice by employing an unlicensed person; administrative citation $500-$5,000 per incident; each day after a cease and desist is a separate violation; civil penalty $500-$5,000 per offense in circuit court — same source
- Florida registrants under 456.47(4) can be disciplined (suspension, revocation, reprimand, letter of concern) — 456.47(4)

### Inferences
- Aiding-and-abetting exposure means the tech company and the professional entity must not route patients to physicians unlicensed (and unregistered) in the patient's state; license-state matching should be a hard gate in the MCP workflow.

### Gaps
- Other states' penalties (CA, TX, NY, IL, WA); federal exposure; corporate practice of medicine (physician-owned entity structure, MSO arrangements) belongs to another researcher.
