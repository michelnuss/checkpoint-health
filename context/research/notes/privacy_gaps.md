## HIPAA basics: covered entity vs business associate, BAA content, minimum necessary, authorization, breach notification, retention vs deletion

### Takeaway
Checkpoint's HIPAA status turns on whom it acts for. If it acts for the consumer, it likely sits outside HIPAA (and inside FTC/state law). If it performs functions for the physician practice (a covered entity) and handles PHI on its behalf, it is a business associate and needs a BAA with the practice. The consumer-to-Checkpoint leg and the Checkpoint-to-practice leg may be legally different, and the practice's records become PHI once the practice holds them.

### Cited Findings
- Covered entity means (1) a health plan, (2) a health care clearinghouse, (3) a health care provider who transmits any health information in electronic form in connection with a HIPAA standard transaction (e.g., electronic billing of insurers). A physician practice that bills insurance electronically is a covered entity; a cash-only practice that never conducts standard transactions may not be — [45 CFR 160.103 (Cornell LII)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/160.103)
- Business associate (para (1)(i)): a person who, on behalf of a covered entity (other than as workforce), "creates, receives, maintains, or transmits protected health information for a function or activity regulated by this subchapter," including claims processing, data analysis, utilization review, quality assurance, billing, practice management. Para (1)(ii) also covers persons providing legal, actuarial, accounting, consulting, data aggregation, management, administrative, accreditation, or financial services to a covered entity where the service involves disclosure of PHI to the person — [45 CFR 160.103](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/160.103)
- Health care provider includes "any other person or organization who furnishes, bills, or is paid for health care in the normal course of business" — [45 CFR 160.103](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/160.103)
- Covered entity may disclose PHI to a business associate only after "satisfactory assurance" documented in a written contract meeting 164.504(e); a BA may likewise disclose to a subcontractor BA with a compliant subcontract — [45 CFR 164.502(e)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/164.502)
- BAA required content (164.504(e)(2)): establish permitted/required uses and disclosures (cannot authorize uses the CE could not make, except BA management/administration and data aggregation); BA must not use or further disclose other than as contract permits or law requires; use appropriate safeguards and comply with Security Rule (subpart C) for ePHI; report improper uses/disclosures including breaches of unsecured PHI (164.410); flow down same restrictions to subcontractors; make PHI available for access (164.524), amendment (164.526), accounting of disclosures (164.528); comply with CE obligations if delegated; make books and records available to HHS; at termination return or destroy PHI if feasible (else extend protections); contract must allow CE to terminate for material violation — [45 CFR 164.504(e)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/164.504)
- Minimum necessary: CE or BA "must make reasonable efforts to limit protected health information to the minimum necessary to accomplish the intended purpose." Exceptions include disclosures to a health care provider for treatment, to the individual, pursuant to an authorization, to HHS, and as required by law — [45 CFR 164.502(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/164.502)
- Valid authorization core elements (164.508(c)(1)): specific description of information; who may disclose; who may receive; purpose of each use/disclosure ("at the request of the individual" suffices when the individual initiates); expiration date or event; signature and date. Required statements (c)(2): right to revoke in writing and how; whether treatment/payment can be conditioned on signing; potential for redisclosure by recipient and loss of HIPAA protection. Separate authorization needed for psychotherapy notes; authorization required for marketing and for sale of PHI (with remuneration statement). Revocation must be in writing and does not undo actions already taken in reliance — [45 CFR 164.508](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/164.508)
- Individual breach notice (164.404): without unreasonable delay and no later than 60 calendar days after discovery; discovery is first day known or reasonably should have been known to any workforce member or agent; content (what happened, types of PHI, steps individuals should take, what entity is doing, contact procedures incl. toll-free number, email, website, or postal address); first-class mail (email if individual agreed); substitute notice rules (10+ individuals: 90-day website posting or major media plus toll-free number active 90 days) — [45 CFR 164.404](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/164.404)
- Other breach provisions (164.406 media for >500 residents of a state, 164.408 HHS notice contemporaneous for 500+ and annual within 60 days after calendar year end for <500, 164.410 BA notifies CE without unreasonable delay and within 60 days, 164.402 four-factor low-probability-of-compromise assessment, 164.414 burden of proof): UNVERIFIED in this session (page fetch did not return them); these are from background knowledge and should be confirmed at https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-C/part-164/subpart-D
- HIPAA documentation retention: policies, procedures, and required documentation must be kept six years "from the date of its creation or the date when it last was in effect, whichever is later" — [45 CFR 164.530(j)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/164.530)
- Florida physician records: must be kept "for a period of at least five years from the last patient contact" — [Fla. Admin. Code 64B8-10.002 (Cornell LII)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/florida/Fla-Admin-Code-Ann-R-64B8-10-002)
- Florida 456.057: patient records may not be furnished to anyone other than the patient, legal representative, or practitioners involved in care without written authorization, except enumerated cases (arranged/provided care with consent, subpoena with notice, research with abstracted data, poison control, DCF investigations); marketing/solicitation use needs a specific written release; records owners must adopt confidentiality/security policies and train employees (subsec 10) and log third-party disclosures (subsec 11). The statute text itself does not set a retention period — [Fla. Stat. 456.057](http://www.leg.state.fl.us/statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&URL=0400-0499/0456/Sections/0456.057.html)

### Inferences
- Likely three-way analysis (UNVERIFIED legal conclusion, needs counsel): (a) Checkpoint is not a covered entity unless it itself furnishes health care or bills; the physician practice is the provider. (b) When Checkpoint collects the consumer's symptoms on the consumer's own initiative and with the consumer's consent, before any practice relationship, it is arguably acting for the consumer, not the practice, so not a BA for that leg (HHS has taken this view for consumer-directed apps in its health app guidance, UNVERIFIED here). (c) Once Checkpoint transmits packets to the practice and returns reviewed answers, and performs functions "on behalf of" the practice (intake, routing, audit trail, hosting records), it is plausibly a BA of the practice. Safest design: sign a BAA with the practice and treat the practice-bound flow as PHI.
- Because the practice holds the clinical record, Florida's five-years-from-last-contact retention (for the physician) conflicts with blanket consumer deletion rights for the practice-held record. Checkpoint's own copies (pseudonymized packets, audit logs) might be deleted on request while practice-held records are retained; the practice owns the medical record retention duty. HIPAA's 6-year rule covers compliance documentation (policies, authorizations, BAAs), not medical records themselves, so the two clocks differ. Consumer-facing deletion promises should be drafted to carve out records the practice must keep by law.
- The minimum necessary standard does not apply to disclosures for treatment to a provider or to disclosures made under authorization, which supports the packet design but redaction is still good practice.

## OCR online tracking technologies guidance and AHA v. Becerra

### Takeaway
On June 20, 2024, the N.D. Tex. vacated the "Proscribed Combination" portion of OCR's bulletin (IP address plus visit to an unauthenticated public health webpage is not automatically PHI). HHS withdrew its appeal on or about Aug 29, 2024, so the vacatur stands; the rest of the bulletin (e.g., tracking on authenticated pages) was not vacated.

### Cited Findings
- Court (N.D. Tex., No. 4:23-cv-1110) declared unlawful and vacated the Proscribed Combination portion, saying the bulletin "improperly creat[ed] substantive legal obligations for covered entities"; ruling June 20, 2024 — [Proskauer](https://proskauer.com/blog/shortly-after-its-online-tracking-technologies-bulletin-is-declared-unlawful-hhs-ocr-stands-down-withdraws-appeal)
- HHS-OCR moved to voluntarily dismiss its appeal on August 29, 2024, about ten days after filing — [Proskauer](https://proskauer.com/blog/shortly-after-its-online-tracking-technologies-bulletin-is-declared-unlawful-hhs-ocr-stands-down-withdraws-appeal)
- The rest of the bulletin (IP addresses and device IDs can be individually identifiable health information; HIPAA obligations for tracking on authenticated pages) was not described as vacated; state wiretap and privacy class actions continue — [Proskauer](https://proskauer.com/blog/shortly-after-its-online-tracking-technologies-bulletin-is-declared-unlawful-hhs-ocr-stands-down-withdraws-appeal)
- Additional coverage (search results only, not fetched): [Holland & Knight](https://hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2024/06/american-hospital-assn-v-becerra-are-tracking-tools-ok-again), [AHA](https://aha.org/node/694014), [Baird Holm](https://bairdholm.com/blog/ocrs-online-tracking-technology-bulletin-aha-lawsuit-scores-a-win-hhs-withdraws-appeal)

### Inferences
- For Checkpoint, vacatur gives little comfort: the FTC (GoodRx, BetterHelp, Premom, Cerebral) and state wiretap/health-privacy laws independently reach pixel/SDK sharing. Recommendation: no third-party ad/analytics trackers on symptom-intake surfaces.

## FTC Health Breach Notification Rule (16 CFR Part 318) and FTC enforcement

### Takeaway
As amended (final rule published May 30, 2024, 89 FR 47028 per the Federal Register search hit, effective July 29, 2024, UNVERIFIED date), the HBNR covers non-HIPAA health apps and services, and treats unauthorized disclosure (e.g., sharing with advertisers) as a "breach of security." A Checkpoint consumer-facing layer not governed by HIPAA is a likely "vendor of personal health records."

### Cited Findings
- "Breach of security": unauthorized acquisition of unsecured PHR identifiable health information in a personal health record; presumed to include unauthorized access absent reliable evidence otherwise; covers acquisitions from a data breach or an unauthorized disclosure — [16 CFR 318.2 (Cornell LII)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/318.2)
- "Vendor of personal health records": an entity, other than a HIPAA covered entity or a business associate acting as one, that offers or maintains a personal health record. PHR = electronic record of PHR identifiable health information with technical capacity to draw from multiple sources, managed/shared/controlled by or primarily for the individual — [16 CFR 318.2](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/318.2)
- "PHR related entity" and "health care services or supplies" (any online service/app/device that provides mechanisms to track diseases, treatments, medications, vital signs, fitness, fertility, sleep, mental health, genetic info, diet, or other health-related tools) were added/clarified in the 2024 amendments — [16 CFR 318.2](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/318.2)
- Timing: notice to individuals (and media where applicable) without unreasonable delay and no later than 60 calendar days after discovery. FTC notice contemporaneous with individual notice for breaches of 500 or more individuals; for fewer than 500, annual log submitted within 60 days after calendar year end. Burden on entity to prove timeliness. Law-enforcement delay allowed — [16 CFR 318.4](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/318.4)
- Enforcement: violation treated as violation of a rule under section 18 of the FTC Act (civil penalties, inflation-adjusted per 16 CFR 1.98) — [16 CFR 318.7](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/318.7). Current per-violation amount (about $53,088 in 2025): UNVERIFIED, check 16 CFR 1.98 for 2026.
- Federal Register final rule: https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/05/30/2024-10855/health-breach-notification-rule (fetch blocked; appears in search results; also [govinfo PDF](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2024-05-30/pdf/2024-10855.pdf)); law-firm summaries: [WSGR](https://wsgr.com/en/insights/ftc-final-rule-officially-broadens-health-breach-notification-rule-targets-health-and-wellness-apps.html), [Venable](https://www.venable.com/insights/publications/2024/07/final-changes-to-health-breach-notification)
- GoodRx: $1.5 million civil penalty, announced Feb 1, 2023; first HBNR action; theory: intentionally sharing PHR identifiable health information with advertisers such as Facebook without authorization is a breach of security requiring notice; also barred from sharing health data for most advertising, privacy program required; seven Section 5 counts — [Arnold & Porter](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/blogs/enforcement-edge/2023/02/ftc-fines-goodrx)
- BetterHelp: $7.8 million paid as consumer refunds (users who paid Aug 1, 2017 to Dec 31, 2020); ban on sharing health data for advertising; affirmative express consent before disclosures to certain third parties; third parties must delete data; privacy program and retention schedule; announced March 2, 2023 (proposed order, Commission vote 4-0) — [FTC press release](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/03/ftc-ban-betterhelp-revealing-consumers-data-including-sensitive-mental-health-information-facebook). Note BetterHelp was a Section 5 case, not an HBNR case (background knowledge, UNVERIFIED here).
- Premom (Easy Healthcare): $100,000 civil penalty judgment (statement filed May 17, 2023, Case 1:23-cv-03107), alleged violations of FTC Act and HBNR, permanent injunction, ban on sharing certain information, comprehensive privacy and security program — [FTC statement of reasons PDF](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/2023186easyhealthcarereasonforsettlement.pdf). HIPAA Journal headline mentions "$200,000 penalty" — the FTC civil penalty was $100,000; the additional amount reflects state AG settlements (Connecticut, Oregon, D.C.) (UNVERIFIED breakdown) — [HIPAA Journal](https://www.hipaajournal.com/200000-penalty-for-impermissible-sharing-of-premom-app-users-health-data/)
- Cerebral: about $7 million total (April 2024), reported as roughly $5.1 million in refunds plus a $10 million civil penalty suspended to about $2 million due to inability to pay (the common press formulation); the FTC order bans using health data for most advertising. Amount details UNVERIFIED: search hits only ([Becker's](https://www.beckershospitalreview.com/telehealth/ftc-slaps-cerebral-with-7m-fine-for-privacy-violations.html), [Security Magazine](https://www.securitymagazine.com/articles/100609-ftc-orders-cerebral-to-restrict-how-consumer-data-can-be-shared)); confirm at ftc.gov before citing.

### Inferences
- Because "breach" includes voluntary disclosure to a third party without consumer authorization, a Checkpoint integration that shared data with analytics vendors or the LLM provider beyond what the consent covered could trigger HBNR notice duties. Clear, specific, affirmative consent for each recipient (physician practice, AI vendor, cloud host) reduces this risk.
- HBNR excludes HIPAA covered entities and BAs acting as such, so which hat Checkpoint wears for each data flow matters; it may be a dual-status entity.

## State laws: California (CMIA, CCPA/CPRA), Texas (HB 300, TDPSA), Florida (FDBR, FIPA)

### Takeaway
Florida's general privacy statute (FDBR) is very narrow (billion-dollar thresholds), so Florida exposure is chiefly FIPA breach notice, 456.057 confidentiality, and wiretap law. Texas HB 300 applies broadly to anyone who handles PHI, and California CMIA reaches digital health apps. Only Florida items below are verified.

### Cited Findings
- FIPA (Fla. Stat. 501.171): personal information includes name plus "medical history, mental or physical condition, or medical treatment or diagnosis by a health care professional" and health insurance identifiers; notice to individuals within 30 days of determining a breach; notice to the Department of Legal Affairs for breaches of 500+ Florida residents within 30 days (15-day extension for good cause); civil penalties of $1,000/day for first 30 days, then $50,000 per subsequent 30-day period up to 180 days, up to $500,000 per breach; no private right of action — [Fla. Stat. 501.171](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2024/501.171) (2024 version; confirm no 2025-26 amendments)
- Florida Digital Bill of Rights (Fla. Stat. 501.701 et seq.): applies to for-profit controllers with over $1 billion global gross annual revenue AND one of: 50% revenue from online ad sales, app store with 250,000+ apps, or smart speaker/voice assistant; health diagnoses are sensitive data requiring consent; HIPAA-covered information exempt; effective July 1, 2024; AG enforcement, up to $50,000 per violation, treble for knowing/willful or certain child violations; no private right — [DWT tracker](https://www.dwt.com/gcp/state-privacy-laws-tracker/broad-state-privacy-law-tracker/florida) (secondary source). Note: one part of the FDBR (sale of sensitive data, 501.715) applies to any for-profit entity doing business in Florida regardless of revenue, requiring consent to sell sensitive data (UNVERIFIED; commonly reported, see [Privacy World](https://www.privacyworld.blog/2023/05/florida-joins-the-privacy-pack-with-an-opt-in-to-sale-of-sensitive-data/)).
- Texas Medical Records Privacy Act (HB 300, Tex. Health & Safety Code ch. 181) exists and amended 2011 (HB 300, 82nd Leg.); statute text: https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/HS/htm/HS.181.htm (not retrieved), bill text https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/82r/billtext/html/hb00300f.htm. Known features, all UNVERIFIED in this session: "covered entity" is defined much more broadly than HIPAA (any person who comes into possession of PHI, including business associates, and persons who assemble, collect, analyze, use, evaluate, store, or transmit PHI); employee training requirement (181.101); consumer notice and authorization for electronic disclosure (181.154); prohibition on sale of PHI (181.153); civil penalties tiered up to $5,000 negligent, $25,000 knowing/intentional, $250,000 for financial gain, and up to $1.5 million annual cap for repeated violations (verify at 181.201).
- Texas TDPSA (Tex. Bus. & Com. Code ch. 541, effective July 1, 2024): UNVERIFIED in this session; reported features: applies to businesses in Texas that process personal data and are not small businesses as defined by SBA, requires consent for sensitive data (incl. health diagnosis), exempts HIPAA covered entities/BAs and PHI; AG enforcement with 30-day cure. Small businesses still must obtain consent before selling sensitive data (reported).
- California CMIA (Civ. Code 56 et seq.) and CCPA/CPRA sensitive personal information: NOT researched in this session due to tool budget; UNVERIFIED background: CMIA 56.05/56.06 extends "provider of health care" to businesses offering software/hardware (including mobile apps) designed to maintain medical information; 56.10 requires authorization for disclosure; 56.35/56.36 provide private right with $1,000 nominal damages; CCPA exempts CMIA-governed medical information and HIPAA PHI but not other health data; CPRA treats health information as sensitive PI with right to limit use. The earlier researcher covered SB 690; confirm CMIA specifics at https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov.

### Inferences
- If Checkpoint's operating entity is small and Florida-based, FDBR likely does not apply by threshold (inference from DWT summary); FIPA and 456.057-type obligations (through the practice) apply.
- Texas HB 300's broad "covered entity" definition likely captures Checkpoint when it handles Texas residents' PHI regardless of HIPAA status (inference, UNVERIFIED), and training/notice obligations would apply.

## 42 CFR Part 2, Wiretap Act, and all-party consent laws (CIPA, Florida 934)

### Takeaway
Part 2 is probably marginal (only applies to federally assisted SUD programs). The bigger exposure is wiretap/eavesdropping claims over chatbot transcripts and embedded vendors, especially in all-party-consent states. None of this section was verified from primary sources in this session (tool failures); treat as background knowledge.

### Cited Findings
- None verified with a fetched source. Attempts to retrieve Cal. Penal Code 631 (leginfo) and Fla. Stat. 934.03 (flsenate) failed.
- UNVERIFIED background, to be confirmed: Part 2 (42 CFR Part 2) protects records of federally assisted substance use disorder programs that hold themselves out as providing SUD diagnosis, treatment, or referral; a symptom-intake service is relevant only if it handles records from such a program or itself is one, and the 2024 final rule aligned Part 2 with HIPAA breach notice and permitted broader TPO consent. Source to cite: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/chapter-I/subchapter-A/part-2.
- UNVERIFIED background: Federal Wiretap Act (18 U.S.C. 2511) is one-party consent but prohibits interception for a criminal or tortious purpose (2511(2)(d)), the exception plaintiffs invoke in pixel cases involving health data; ECPA civil remedy 18 U.S.C. 2520 (greater of $100/day or $10,000 statutory damages).
- UNVERIFIED background: California CIPA Penal Code 631 (wiretapping, aiding a third party to read communications in transit), 632 (confidential communications recording needs all-party consent), 637.2 ($5,000 per violation statutory damages, private right). Courts are split on whether third-party chatbot or analytics vendors "intercept" under 631; the 2024 CIPA 638.51 pen-register theories were also asserted. Florida Security of Communications Act (Fla. Stat. ch. 934, esp. 934.03 interception; 934.10 civil remedy; all-party consent for oral/wire/electronic communications), with civil damages of the greater of actual damages or $100/day or $10,000 (UNVERIFIED).

### Inferences
- Disclosures of recording/transcribing and AI vendor involvement, with a clear affirmative consent click at the start of the conversation, address the all-party consent exposure; the sensitive point is that Checkpoint's MCP is mediated by a third-party AI assistant, so the consumer's chat is already with Anthropic (or another host) before Checkpoint is invoked.

## Anthropic consumer privacy and retention; API zero data retention

### Takeaway
Consumer Claude (Free, Pro, Max) is not HIPAA-eligible and is not under any BAA; retention is 30 days by default, extended to 5 years if the user allows model training; the API has a ZDR arrangement available by agreement, and a separate HIPAA-ready configuration under a signed BAA. A consumer typing symptoms into Claude.ai is governed by the consumer policy, not Checkpoint's own terms.

### Cited Findings
- Consumer terms update published Aug 28, 2025: retention extended to five years "if you allow us to use your data for model training"; 30 days if not opted in; users can change the training setting anytime; turning it off stops future training runs from using new chats but data already used stays in trained models; applies to Claude Free, Pro, Max (including Claude Code on those plans); not to Commercial Terms (Team, Enterprise, API, Bedrock, Vertex, Claude Gov, Education); five-year period also applies to feedback submitted; deleted conversations are not used for future training; existing users had until Oct 8, 2025 to accept — [Anthropic news](https://www.anthropic.com/news/updates-to-our-consumer-terms)
- Retention detail (privacy center): deleted conversations removed from chat history immediately and from back-end storage within 30 days; training opt-in data kept up to 5 years (de-identified, new or resumed chats only); chats flagged under the Usage Policy may keep inputs and outputs up to 2 years and classification scores up to 7 years; feedback data kept 5 years; incognito chats not used for improvement — [Anthropic privacy center](https://privacy.claude.com/en/articles/10023548-how-long-do-you-store-my-data)
- API ZDR: Anthropic does not store prompts or responses at rest after the API response is returned; ZDR is an arrangement requested via sales, enabled per organization (not default); covers Messages and Token Counting APIs for eligible features; does not cover Claude Console, consumer plans, Teams/Enterprise product interfaces, MCP connector, Files API, Batch, code execution, and other listed features; flagged content and legal holds may still be retained (flagged inputs and outputs up to 2 years) — [Claude API docs: ZDR](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/zero-data-retention)
- HIPAA readiness: requires a signed BAA and a HIPAA-enabled organization (standard BAA via Console or custom via sales); a different model from ZDR (safeguards across lifecycle rather than immediate deletion); no need for ZDR in addition; non-eligible features return a 400 error; permanent once enabled; consumer plans, Console processing of PHI, Bedrock, Google Cloud, Claude Code are not covered; PHI must not appear in structured-output JSON schemas — [Claude API docs: ZDR](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/zero-data-retention)

### Inferences
- A consumer who describes symptoms in Claude.ai is outside any BAA; Checkpoint's connector receives data after the user already disclosed it to the assistant provider, so Checkpoint's consent flow should disclose that the host AI assistant's own policy governs that earlier chat. Checkpoint's own server-side LLM use (if any) should go through a HIPAA-enabled API organization and avoid ineligible features (e.g., MCP connector, Files API, Batch).
