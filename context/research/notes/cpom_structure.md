## 1. What is the CPOM doctrine, and how do FL, CA, NY, TX, IL treat it? Which states are strict?

### Takeaway
CPOM is a state-law doctrine that bars lay corporations from practicing medicine or employing physicians, to protect physician independent judgment. More than 30 states have some form of it. Florida is generally viewed as permissive; New York, Texas, California, Oregon and Washington are the strict end; Illinois is mid-to-strict for non-hospital corporations. The strictness ranking is partly a matter of commentary, not statute, and sources differ.

### Cited Findings
- CPOM restricts ownership of medical practices and employment of physicians by entities that are not licensed physicians, PCs or PLLCs; the aim is to keep non-clinicians from influencing professional judgment. — [Epstein Becker Green (2013, dated)](https://www.ebglaw.com/health-law-advisor/corporate-practice-of-medicine-the-unseen-hurdle-in-telehealth)
- "More than 30 states" maintain some form of CPOM prohibition; scope and enforcement vary widely. — [McDermott](https://www.mcdermottlaw.com/insights/under-new-management-not-so-fast-federal-cpom-bill-targets-mso-structures-friendly-physician-model/)
- Florida: no specific statute bars physicians from practicing through a corporate structure; the Florida Board of Medicine has said the unlicensed-practice prohibition does not bar physicians employed by a Florida corporation or partnership. This is from a 2013 article; no 2025-26 Florida-specific confirmation was found. — [EBG](https://www.ebglaw.com/health-law-advisor/corporate-practice-of-medicine-the-unseen-hurdle-in-telehealth)
- California: prohibits CPOM; business or management decisions that control a physician's practice must be made by a licensed California physician. — [EBG](https://www.ebglaw.com/health-law-advisor/corporate-practice-of-medicine-the-unseen-hurdle-in-telehealth)
- Strictest-states ranking (Harris Sliwoski, undated): New York first, Washington second, Texas third, California "honorable mention" as relatively strict. In NY, lay companies can offer only a very limited set of administrative services, and stock restrictions and some physician non-competes may be impermissible. In Texas, any direction or control by a non-physician can create a violation; risky MSO features include profit-sharing, the MSO signing third-party contracts for the PC, and the MSO selecting medical staff. California has an exception letting physicians pay a portion of gross revenues for goods and services if the payment reflects fair market value. That article does not discuss Illinois or Florida. — [Harris Sliwoski](https://harris-sliwoski.com/cannalawblog/ketamine-ventures-the-strictest-corporate-practice-of-medicine-states/)
- Texas (vendor blog, Mar 2025, updated Nov 2025; not primary): non-physicians cannot practice medicine or employ physicians, control hiring, firing, salary or fees; Texas Medical Board "actively enforces"; MSO cannot influence medical decisions, pay or patient fees; warns against revenue-sharing; cites Tex. Occ. Code 155.001, 155.003, 157.001, 165.156 [VERIFY]. — [Zivian Health](https://zivianhealth.com/blog/corporate-practice-of-medicine-cpom-guide-texas/)
- Texas has statutory exemptions (e.g., certain non-profit health organizations under Occ. Code ch. 162, per TMB page; Texas AG opinion JC-0304 (2000) in search results addressed this). Not fetched in detail. — [TMB non-profit health orgs](https://tmb.state.tx.us/page/non-profit-health-organizations); [Tex. AG JC-0304](https://www.texasattorneygeneral.gov/sites/default/files/opinion-files/opinion/2000/jc0304.pdf)
- Illinois: doctrine originates in People ex rel. Kerner v. United Medical Service (1936), reasoning that no corporation can meet the license requirements. Berlin v. Sarah Bush Lincoln Health Center (Ill. 1997) carved out licensed hospitals only; the doctrine remains for other corporations. — [Berlin, Ill. S. Ct.](https://law.justia.com/cases/illinois/supreme-court/1997/81059.html)
- Illinois Medical Practice Act fee-splitting section is 225 ILCS 60/22.2 (not read; listed in search results). — [FindLaw](https://codes.findlaw.com/il/chapter-225-professionsoccupations-and-business-operations/il-st-sect-225-60-22-2/) [VERIFY]
- New York: sources indicate percentage-based fee splitting between physicians and lay owners is unlawful, with AG and court action (Aspen Dental settlement; Second Dept 2014 fee-splitting decision). Not read in full. — [Schlam Stone summary](https://www.schlamstone.com/blogs/commercial/2014-10-16-second-department-affirms-decision-finding-fee-splitting-agreements-unlawful); [Benesch on Aspen](https://www.beneschlaw.com/insight/aspen-dental-management-settles-with-n-y-ags-office-to-end-investigation-into-unauthorized-practice-of-dentistry/) [VERIFY]
- Enforcement is inconsistent; the issue most often surfaces in commercial disputes where physician owners argue MSO agreements are unenforceable as CPOM. — [EBG](https://www.ebglaw.com/health-law-advisor/corporate-practice-of-medicine-the-unseen-hurdle-in-telehealth)
- 2025-26 changes: California SB 351 (signed Oct 6, 2025, effective Jan 1, 2026) bars private equity groups and hedge funds from interfering with physician/dentist professional judgment and from controlling specified activities such as clinical staffing; bars noncompete and nondisparagement clauses in management contracts with PE-controlled entities. AB 1415 (signed Oct 11, 2025, effective Jan 1, 2026) makes MSOs "noticing entities" that must give OHCA notice of certain material transactions. — [Cooley, Dec 2025](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2025/2025-12-16-californias-new-laws-what-private-equity-needs-to-know-about-healthcare-investment-restrictions). Note: SB 351 targets PE/hedge funds; whether a venture-backed tech startup is covered depends on definitions [VERIFY].
- Oregon SB 951 (signed June 9, 2025): PC owners/directors/officers generally cannot own, direct or work for the MSO; MSOs cannot hold ultimate authority over hiring, firing, schedules, compensation, clinical staffing, diagnostic coding, pricing, billing policies or payor contracts; equity transfer restrictions and many non-competes limited. Compliance Jan 1, 2026 for new entities and Jan 1, 2029 for pre-existing. Telemedicine entities with no Oregon location where patients receive clinical services (apart from DEA-registration sites) get an exception from the ownership limits. Oregon later added exceptions after stakeholder input (McDermott), so check current text. — [Foley](https://www.foley.com/?p=113615); [McDermott](https://www.mcdermottlaw.com/insights/under-new-management-not-so-fast-federal-cpom-bill-targets-mso-structures-friendly-physician-model/)
- Federal: Stop Corporate Takeovers of Physicians Act of 2026 (H.R. 10444), introduced Sept 16, 2026 (Wyden/Merkley/Warren; Hoyle/Ocasio-Cortez/Subramanyam). Would create a first federal CPOM ban; entities not majority owned and controlled by licensees could not own practices or employ/contract for licensee services; licensee owners must be licensed and practicing in each state where the practice serves patients; bars MSO de facto control and MSO equity; MSO fees must be arm's-length and FMV; no grandfathering after one year; FTC, state AG and private treble-damages enforcement. Introduced only; Simpson Thacher says unlikely to advance this Congress. No telehealth exception described. — [Simpson Thacher, Sept 25 2026](https://www.stblaw.com/about-us/publications/view/2026/09/25/stop-corporate-takeovers-of-physicians-act-of-2026-bill-would-establish-a-federal-corporate-practice-of-medicine-ban-and-restrict-management-services-organization-arrangements); [McDermott](https://www.mcdermottlaw.com/insights/under-new-management-not-so-fast-federal-cpom-bill-targets-mso-structures-friendly-physician-model/)
- Holland & Knight scheduled an Oct 2026 event titled around "Physician vs. MSO Disputes: Unwinding of the Friendly PC Relationship in the Midst of the CPOM Crackdown," signaling market perception of a crackdown (title only). — [Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/events/2026/10/physician-vs-mso-disputes-navigating-the-unwinding-of-the-friendly-pc)

### Inferences
- Checkpoint should choose the PC state(s) by where patients are located (CPOM and licensure attach to the patient's state in telehealth) and design to the strictest state it serves (NY, TX, CA, OR, WA), or geofence those states at launch.
- The H.R. 10444 "licensed and practicing in each state" requirement would, if enacted, undercut a single-owner multistate PC; low odds near term but design with it in mind.
- Florida being permissive does not help patients in other states, so a Florida-only entity does not solve multistate CPOM.

## 2. MSO + friendly PC model, management services agreement features, how K Health, Teladoc, Included Health, Amazon/SteadyMD structure it, and risks

### Takeaway
The standard design: a physician-owned PC/PA/PLLC employs or contracts with the clinicians and holds the patient relationship; the tech company (MSO) provides administration, technology and billing under a management services agreement (MSA), with the MSO insulated from clinical decisions. All four peers' terms of service disclaim practicing medicine and point to affiliated professional entities. Teladoc's contract uses fixed fees rather than a percentage, which is the conservative model.

### Cited Findings
- Structure: physician-owned PC/PLLC employs licensed professionals; contracts with an MSO for administrative and management services; aligned with MSO through stock transfer restriction agreements, MSO employment of the physician owner, or both. Risk is that heavy control becomes a sham covering de facto unlicensed practice; factors include control over decisions and revenue extraction. — [EBG (2013)](https://www.ebglaw.com/health-law-advisor/corporate-practice-of-medicine-the-unseen-hurdle-in-telehealth)
- Features regulators and legislators now target: equity transfer restriction agreements, dual roles across PC and MSO, MSO involvement in practice formation and acquisition financing, MSO control over staffing, compensation, billing and payor contracting, and nominal physician owners. — [McDermott](https://www.mcdermottlaw.com/insights/under-new-management-not-so-fast-federal-cpom-bill-targets-mso-structures-friendly-physician-model/)
- Teladoc (2015 Amended and Restated Services Agreement between Teladoc, Inc. and Teladoc Physicians, P.A.): fixed monthly management fee ($15,000, rising to $65,000 if consults exceed 25,000 per month), flat $10,000 monthly trade-name license fee, direct costs reimbursed; PA/PCs retain control of telehealth services and medical decisions (s.1.4); Teladoc, Inc. does not practice medicine and will not influence physician judgment (s.1.5); Teladoc manages non-medical functions (technology, queue, call center, billing, non-medical staffing); PA/PC sets physician hiring, pay and discharge and physicians are on the PA/PC payroll (s.5.1); Teladoc may recommend staffing levels (s.5.2); shortfall funding is accrued with interest and secured by the PA's assets. Note the tier step-up at 25,000 consults is volume-linked, a mild flag. — [Justia contract](https://contracts.justia.com/companies/teladoc-health-inc-3746/contract/441974)
- Teladoc current consumer ToS: certain programs (chronic condition management, digital behavioral health, expert medical services) "do not create a doctor-patient relationship between you and Teladoc." The fetched page did not discuss Teladoc Health Physicians P.A. — [Teladoc ToS](https://www.teladochealth.com/terms-of-service/)
- K Health ToU: Company "is not a provider of clinical advice or a health care provider"; "K Health Affiliates" are K Health's affiliated professional corporations; Providers are employed or contracted by them; providers act "in their own discretion and professional judgment"; provider-patient relationship is only between user and Provider; affiliates and providers "may have a financial interest in K Health, Inc."; AI symptom checker assessments "do not constitute clinical care, advice or diagnosis." — [K Health Terms of Use](https://khealth.com/terms-of-use)
- Included Health ToS (Mar 2026 version): "Included Health does not engage in the practice of medicine"; clinical care delivered through Doctor On Demand Professionals, PC, a network of independent physician-owned medical groups, not employees; providers solely responsible for care; Included Health "does not direct or interfere" with clinical decisions; for Expert Medical Opinion no physician-patient relationship is created. — [Included Health ToS](https://includedhealth.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Included-Health-Terms-of-Service-_032526-2.pdf)
- Amazon Clinic/SteadyMD: Amazon VHS "does not practice medicine, and does not influence the practice of medicine" by SteadyMD clinicians; patient agreement is with SteadyMD Physician Group, P.C. (a Missouri professional corporation) and contractual affiliates; SteadyMD, Inc. is separate, not licensed, and provides administrative and technology support; the Practice is an independent contractor with "exclusive control" over its work. — [Amazon/SteadyMD agreement](https://clinic.amazon.com/legal/steadymd)
- Typical MSA features visible in the sources: fixed or FMV fees, PC retains clinical control, MSO limited to non-clinical functions, PC controls physician hiring/pay, trade-name license, assignment limits. Features to avoid or scrutinize: percentage-of-revenue fees, sweeps of PC bank accounts, MSO power over fees/staffing/payor contracts, stock-transfer "kill switches," non-competes (void or limited in OR, CA PE-context, and the federal bill). — synthesis of [Teladoc agreement](https://contracts.justia.com/companies/teladoc-health-inc-3746/contract/441974), [Foley](https://www.foley.com/?p=113615), [Stevens & Lee](https://www.stevenslee.com/health-law-observer-blog/management-fee-structures-and-the-corporate-practice-of-medicine/)
- Medical-expert-opinion style services (Included Health) explicitly say no physician-patient relationship forms. This may be a relevant model for an asynchronous "review" product but is a double-edged position, since Checkpoint's reviews give patient-specific advice; see Inferences. — [Included Health ToS](https://includedhealth.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Included-Health-Terms-of-Service-_032526-2.pdf)

### Inferences
- The peers show a consistent pattern: platform entity disclaims medicine; named affiliated PC is the treating party; PC is "independent." Checkpoint's ToS should copy that pattern, adjusted to its facts.
- If Checkpoint's physicians give patient-specific advice (even asynchronously, through an AI assistant), assume a physician-patient relationship and licensure in the patient's state is required; do not rely on "expert opinion, no relationship" language without counsel review (also a licensure question for another researcher).
- Also note: K Health discloses affiliated entities "may have a financial interest" in the platform, a red flag-in-waiting that shows the nominal-owner model is standard but under pressure.

- K Health ToU fetch was truncated at ~100k of 122k characters; the full text may name specific entities (e.g., K Health Medical Group) not captured.
- No MSA terms for K Health, Included Health or SteadyMD were found.

## 3. Fee-splitting rules (Florida and elsewhere); percentage-of-revenue vs flat fee per review

### Takeaway
Florida's Medical Practice Act bars split-fee arrangements "in any form whatsoever" tied to referrals, and the Board of Medicine has historically objected to percentage-of-revenue management fees, while accepting reasonable flat fees. Many CPOM states are stricter. A flat, FMV, non-volume-linked fee for the MSO, and a flat per-review payment to physicians, is the safest design, but note a per-review fee to an MSO is arguably volume-linked, so keep it FMV and documented.

### Cited Findings
- Fla. Stat. 458.331(1)(i) bars "split-fee arrangement in any form whatsoever" for patient referrals; Board of Medicine declaratory statements (1987-1997) permitted fees not tied to referrals (Lundy: 40% of collections for space, equipment, billing and advertising allowed because not tied to referrals; Lozito) but prohibited percentage arrangements that indirectly pay for referrals (Zeterberg, Green Clinic 54%, Crow); Bakarania (1997): "payment of a percentage of the revenue the management services and practice enhancement generate is not permissible," while a reasonable flat management fee was treated as acceptable (order stayed pending appeal). The article is from 1998; current Board positions not confirmed. — [Florida Bar Journal](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/fee-splitting-and-the-management-of-medical-practices-a-history-of-board-of-medicine-declaratory-s/)
- Sources disagree/unsettled: Lundy (40% of collections allowed) vs Bakarania (percentage not permissible); the difference turns on whether the payment is for referrals/marketing. — same source
- Fla. Stat. 456.054: "kickback" is remuneration by or for a provider to induce patient referrals that is not tax-deductible as an ordinary business expense; providers may not offer, pay, solicit or receive kickbacks for referrals; violations are treated as patient brokering under 817.505. — [Fla. Stat. 456.054](https://flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2025/456.054)
- Strict CPOM states may bar percentage-of-revenue fees outright; others regulate them via fee-splitting or referral-payment rules, some applying them to MSO marketing services; flat predetermined fee is "the most conservative option"; percentage and automatic sweep structures carry the highest risk. (Stevens & Lee, Aug 3 2026.) — [Stevens & Lee](https://www.stevenslee.com/health-law-observer-blog/management-fee-structures-and-the-corporate-practice-of-medicine/)
- California exception: physicians may, under conditions, pay a portion of gross revenues for goods and services if FMV. — [Harris Sliwoski](https://harris-sliwoski.com/cannalawblog/ketamine-ventures-the-strictest-corporate-practice-of-medicine-states/)
- New York: percentage-based fee splitting between physicians and lay businesses has been held unlawful in court decisions (Second Dept. 2014) [VERIFY with primary]. — [Schlam Stone](https://www.schlamstone.com/blogs/commercial/2014-10-16-second-department-affirms-decision-finding-fee-splitting-agreements-unlawful)
- H.R. 10444 would require MSO fees at arm's length and FMV as determined by FTC (bill status: introduced). — [Simpson Thacher](https://www.stblaw.com/about-us/publications/view/2026/09/25/stop-corporate-takeovers-of-physicians-act-of-2026-bill-would-establish-a-federal-corporate-practice-of-medicine-ban-and-restrict-management-services-organization-arrangements)
- Teladoc's flat monthly fee structure is a real-world precedent for fixed-fee MSAs. — [Justia](https://contracts.justia.com/companies/teladoc-health-inc-3746/contract/441974)

### Inferences
- Design: (a) patients/agent operators pay the PC (or a billing agent collecting on the PC's behalf); (b) PC pays the MSO a flat monthly fee (or FMV tiered fee) for services and technology license, supported by third-party valuation; (c) PC pays physicians a flat fee per review set by the PC, not by the MSO. Avoid bank sweeps and percentage splits. Per-review pricing is not fee splitting per se, because the physician's fee is not shared with a referral source, but flag volume-based tiers.
- Because Checkpoint's platform "routes" questions from AI assistants to physicians, it is a referral-like source; keep payments from the PC to the MSO unlinked to the number of routed cases, or make them FMV fixed fees for services actually rendered (a documented rationale). This is an inference, not a cited rule.
- If Checkpoint charges AI-agent operators (B2B) per review, structure the operator's payment as for the platform's services, not as payment to the physicians for referral volume.

- Illinois 225 ILCS 60/22.2 and NY Education Law 6530(19)/8 NYCRR 29.1 text not read.

## 4. Federal AKS and Stark; Florida Patient Brokering Act; state kickback and self-referral laws

### Takeaway
The federal AKS applies only where federal health care program business is involved; a purely cash-pay consumer service is largely outside it, but Florida's Patient Brokering Act and state all-payor kickback/fee-splitting laws apply regardless of payer. Stark applies to Medicare/Medicaid-reimbursed designated health services and is unlikely to be relevant to a cash-pay physician review service. Contracts with health systems or any billing of insurance changes the analysis.

### Cited Findings
- Fla. Stat. 817.505 (Patient Brokering Act) makes it unlawful for anyone to offer, pay, solicit or receive kickbacks, bribes or rebates, in cash or in kind, for referring patients or patronage, and covers split-fee arrangements. Exceptions include payments permitted under 42 U.S.C. 1320a-7b(b) (the federal AKS and safe harbors), group practice arrangements, payments for professional consultation services, lawful insurance commissions, and payments by health insurers for covered services, and certain payments to free consumer information services meeting conditions (not steering consumers, not providing diagnostic services, fixed fair-market fees). Penalties: third-degree felony and $50,000 fine for any violation; second-degree (10 to 19 patients) $100,000; first-degree (20+ patients) $500,000. AG or state attorney can seek injunction. It applies to all payers, including cash. — [Fla. Stat. 817.505 (public.law)](https://florida.public.law/statutes/fla._stat._817.505)
- Note on the "free consumer information service" exception: it requires fixed FMV fees and no diagnostic services, so it likely does not protect a service that includes physician review. — inference from the text above.
- Florida's 2019 amendments to the Patient Brokering Act were described as creating uncertainty; the Bradley and AHLA alerts exist but were not read. — [Bradley 2019](https://www.bradley.com/-/media/files/insights/publications/2019/09/significant-changes-to-floridas-patient-brokering-act-uncertainty-lies-ahead.pdf) [VERIFY]
- OIG Special Fraud Alert on telemedicine (July 2022) warned practitioners to apply extra scrutiny to arrangements with telemedicine companies, citing kickback schemes generating medically unnecessary DME, genetic tests, wound care items and prescriptions, and "corruption of medical decision-making"; it states it is not intended to discourage legitimate telemedicine. Summary only; I did not retrieve the alert's list of suspect characteristics (which, from my background knowledge, includes compensation based on volume of items ordered and limited patient interaction) [VERIFY]. — [Nelson Mullins](https://www.nelsonmullins.com/idea_exchange/insights/telemedicine-arrangements-oig-special-fraud-alert)
- OIG Advisory Opinion 25-03 (June 11, 2025): favorable opinion on a PC/MSO arrangement in which a PC leased clinicians from telehealth platforms and paid platforms an additional fee for administrative services regardless of payor reimbursement; OIG found AKS implicated but protected by the personal services and management contracts safe harbor and the outcomes-based payment safe harbor; fees certified as FMV by independent valuator; fees paid regardless of reimbursement made volume/value of referrals less likely to affect fees; opinion limited to its facts. A directly relevant precedent for PC/MSO fixed FMV fees. — [JD Supra summary](https://production.jdsupra.com/legalnews/oig-issues-favorable-advisory-opinion-7170176) [VERIFY the AO number and safe harbors on oig.hhs.gov]
- Cash-pay point: the federal AKS reaches remuneration to induce referrals of items or services "payable by a Federal health care program." No source was retrieved that states this scope explicitly; stated from background knowledge. [VERIFY]
- Stark Law (42 U.S.C. 1395nn) governs physician self-referrals of Medicare/Medicaid designated health services to entities with a financial relationship; not researched through sources in this pass.
- Florida Patient Self-Referral Act of 1992 (Fla. Stat. 456.053) is Florida's all-payor self-referral law [from background knowledge; not retrieved] [VERIFY].

### Inferences
- If Checkpoint (a) stays strictly cash-pay/direct-to-consumer or operator-pays, (b) does not bill Medicare, Medicaid or Tricare, and (c) does not contract with health systems that bill federal programs for downstream care, the federal AKS and Stark are largely not triggered, but state all-payor laws (FL 456.054, 817.505, 458.331(1)(i); other states' fee-splitting and anti-kickback laws) still apply and should drive design.
- Referral-out risk: if Checkpoint ever directs patients to labs, pharmacies, telehealth prescribers or health systems for fees or revenue share, that is the highest-risk behavior under AKS and Florida law. Recommend no payment to or from downstream providers for referral.
- Marketing/affiliate payments (e.g., to AI-agent operators for routing patients, or payments to Anthropic-style platform partners per patient) could be characterized as payment for patient referrals; keep them as flat FMV platform/advertising fees and consult counsel. This is an inference.
- If Checkpoint later accepts insurance or contracts with health systems, build AKS safe harbor compliance (personal services/management contracts, outcomes-based) per AO 25-03 pattern.

- No state anti-kickback/self-referral survey retrieved.

## 5. Must the AI vendor/platform avoid influencing clinical judgment? Design features that preserve physician independence

### Takeaway
Yes, in CPOM states the lay entity must not control or influence clinical judgment, and 2025-26 AI-in-healthcare laws add requirements that practitioners personally review AI output and disclose its use. Peer contracts all contain express non-interference covenants. Specific design guardrails below are drawn from these sources; the list of design features is largely inference.

### Cited Findings
- Texas SB 1188 (effective Sept 1, 2025): practitioners may use AI for diagnosis or treatment if acting within license scope and personally reviewing all AI-generated content or recommendations before making a clinical decision; must disclose AI use to patients. — [JD Supra](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/new-year-new-ai-rules-healthcare-ai-9758831/)
- Texas TRAIGA (effective Jan 1, 2026): licensed practitioners must give conspicuous written disclosure of AI use in diagnosis or treatment; AG enforcement with civil penalties $10,000-$200,000 per violation. — same source
- California AB 489 (effective Jan 1, 2026): AI developers and deployers cannot use terms, letters or design elements suggesting the AI holds a healthcare license; boards can seek injunctions. California AB 3030 and SB 1120 effective January 2025. — same source
- A December 11, 2025 executive order seeks to preempt state AI laws and directs federal challenges; per that source it does not immediately invalidate state laws. Status unsettled. — same source
- Peer contract language: Teladoc agreement s.1.4-1.5 (PA keeps control; Teladoc will not influence professional judgment); Amazon/SteadyMD ("does not influence the practice of medicine"; Practice has "exclusive control"); Included Health ("does not direct or interfere"); K Health (provider decides in own discretion and judgment; may refuse treatment). — sources in section 2.
- Regulators/legislation focus on MSO control of staffing, compensation, billing, payor contracting and "de facto control" over clinical decisions or care quality. — [Foley (Oregon)](https://www.foley.com/?p=113615); [Simpson Thacher (federal bill)](https://www.stblaw.com/about-us/publications/view/2026/09/25/stop-corporate-takeovers-of-physicians-act-of-2026-bill-would-establish-a-federal-corporate-practice-of-medicine-ban-and-restrict-management-services-organization-arrangements)
- OIG alert flags "corruption of medical decision-making" in telemedicine company arrangements. — [Nelson Mullins](https://www.nelsonmullins.com/idea_exchange/insights/telemedicine-arrangements-oig-special-fraud-alert)

### Inferences (design features; none is a cited legal requirement)
- Physician can accept, edit, reject or escalate any AI-prepared packet; the packet is labeled as AI-generated draft input, not a recommendation; the physician must be able to request more information and decline to answer (e.g., refer to in-person or emergency care) without penalty.
- No quotas, minimum review counts, time-per-case targets, queue timers or throughput-based pay tiers imposed by the MSO; clinical review standards and protocols set by the PC's medical director, not the MSO; QA done by the PC.
- AI configuration and updates affecting clinical content approved by the PC's medical director; MSO supplies tooling but does not set clinical thresholds; document the governance.
- Physician compensation set by the PC; flat per-review pay with no incentive tied to approval rates, upsell or downstream orders.
- Disclose AI use to patients (TX SB 1188/TRAIGA) and avoid AI persona implying licensure (CA AB 489), including in the MCP connector's UX and outputs.
- Keep an audit trail showing physician review actually occurred (time spent, edits), supporting the TX "personal review" requirement.

### Gaps
- No FSMB/AMA AI guidance, Florida Board of Medicine AI position, or FDA CDS analysis retrieved here (assumed covered by the FDA/AI researcher; see fda_ai_laws.md).
- No authority found that directly states an AI vendor must not influence clinical judgment; the requirement derives from CPOM principles and peer contracts.
- Illinois and New York AI-specific laws not researched.

## 6. Physician employment vs independent contractor classification

### Takeaway
Under the standard model, physicians are employed by or contracted to the PC (not the platform), and misclassification claims against telehealth companies are rising: a Feb 2026 putative class action targets per-patient-paid contracted telehealth doctors. Classification should be decided by the PC with employment counsel; the platform should not control how physicians work.

### Cited Findings
- Cioppettini v. Mochi Medical CA, P.C., No. 3:26-cv-01260 (N.D. Cal., filed Feb 11, 2026): a physician paid per patient per month alleges misclassification as an independent contractor, seeks California class and nationwide collective; claims include unpaid overtime, wage statement violations, unreimbursed expenses. Article describes the California ABC test (control; usual course of business; independently established business) and notes plaintiff argues all three prongs fail. The article does not address Borello or the physician exemption. — [Reed Smith, Feb 24 2026](https://www.reedsmith.com/articles/worker-misclassification-in-telehealth-new-california-class-action-signals-litigation-risk-for-contractor-arrangements/)
- Other misclassification coverage exists (Mondaq, Miller Shah, Foley) but was not read in this pass. — [Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/unitedstates/employee-rights-labour-relations/1667060/are-telehealth-physicians-being-misclassified-as-independent-contractors-key-warning-signs-for-virtual-care-providers)
- In Teladoc's agreement, physicians are on the PA/PC payroll, not Teladoc Inc.'s; PC sets hiring, pay and discharge. — [Justia](https://contracts.justia.com/companies/teladoc-health-inc-3746/contract/441974)

### Inferences
- Because the "usual course of business" prong favors employee status for per-review physicians doing the core service, expect risk for 1099 treatment in ABC-test states; California's AB 5 / AB 2257 physician-surgeon exemption (from background knowledge: physicians are generally exempt from the ABC test and use the Borello test) [VERIFY] may mitigate for MDs/DOs, so verify.
- Independent contractor status for physicians relative to the PC is common, but the Checkpoint MSO should not set hours, require shifts, or monitor throughput, which is also what CPOM design wants. The two analyses (CPOM independence vs. employment control) point the same direction.
- A per-review pay model fits contractor status better than hourly pay, but fixed schedules or minimum availability would cut against it.

- No Florida-specific classification authority found.

## 7. Consumer terms of service: "platform does not practice medicine" and related terms

### Takeaway
Peer ToS share a template; Checkpoint should mirror it but make sure the facts match the words, because a ToS disclaimer will not save a structure that in reality controls clinical decisions.

### Cited Findings (clauses observed in peers)
- Platform not a provider of clinical advice/does not practice medicine; names affiliated professional entity as the party providing medical services. — [K Health](https://khealth.com/terms-of-use); [Included Health](https://includedhealth.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Included-Health-Terms-of-Service-_032526-2.pdf); [Amazon/SteadyMD](https://clinic.amazon.com/legal/steadymd)
- Provider-patient relationship only between patient and provider; advice comes from the provider, not the company. — [K Health](https://khealth.com/terms-of-use)
- Disclosure that providers/affiliates may have financial interest in the company. — [K Health](https://khealth.com/terms-of-use)
- Providers decide in their independent judgment whether to treat or prescribe and may refuse. — [K Health](https://khealth.com/terms-of-use)
- Providers responsible for their own licensure and quality; patient complaints to state licensing board; emergencies call 911; clinical records kept on behalf of the professional entity. — [Included Health](https://includedhealth.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Included-Health-Terms-of-Service-_032526-2.pdf)
- Company disclaims liability for acts or omissions of providers. — [K Health](https://khealth.com/terms-of-use)
- The affiliated Practice is an independent contractor with exclusive control over its work; platform provides admin and technology. — [Amazon/SteadyMD](https://clinic.amazon.com/legal/steadymd)

### Inferences (suggested Checkpoint clauses)
- Identify the PC(s) by name and state; state that Checkpoint, Inc. is not a medical provider, does not practice medicine, and does not control physicians' professional judgment.
- State the AI role plainly: the AI assistant is not a physician; AI-prepared case packets are inputs; a licensed physician decides what advice to give; the physician may decline or refer; disclose AI use (TX).
- State that reviews are asynchronous, not emergency care; 911 language; limits (no diagnosis for emergencies, state availability).
- Disclose financial relationship between the PC owners and Checkpoint.
- Clarify whether a physician-patient relationship forms with the reviewing physician (avoid "no relationship" language unless counsel confirms the service is truly general information).
- Operator (B2B) terms: operator cannot dictate clinical content or outcomes of reviews and must not market the AI as a licensed clinician.
