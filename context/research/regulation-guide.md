# Checkpoint Can Launch Lawfully, But Only Narrowly

**This is research, not legal advice.** It synthesizes seven sets of research notes current to November 7, 2026. Many primary sources (statutes, CFR sections, FDA guidance) were reached only through law firm summaries, mirror sites or search snippets, and the notes say so. Nothing here substitutes for a licensed Florida health care lawyer, and the final section lists what the team should have a lawyer check.

Checkpoint is legally buildable as a US-first, Florida-first product, provided the clinical act is performed by a licensed physician working through a physician-owned practice, and the software never presents itself as the doctor. The binding constraints are not exotic AI rules. They are old ones: a physician must be licensed in the state where the patient sits, a lay company generally cannot employ or control physicians (corporate practice of medicine, CPOM), and consumer health data held outside HIPAA is policed by the FTC and state privacy laws, which have produced the sector's actual enforcement (GoodRx, BetterHelp, Cerebral, and the July 2026 Hims & Hers suit). Florida is the friendliest launch state: its telehealth statute expressly allows asynchronous care, and Florida CPOM is viewed as permissive. The cleanest scope excludes controlled substances, medication changes, mental-health output and emergencies. The cleanest corporate design is a Florida physician-owned practice (PC) that employs the doctors, paired with Checkpoint as a management services organization (MSO) on a flat fair-market-value fee. The riskiest part of the vision is the later international phase: no source found shows a foreign-licensed doctor can lawfully advise a US patient on their own, so India and Brazil doctors should initially do only non-clinical work, or act as documented consultants to a US doctor of record, subject to local counsel. Several points below are contested between sources or unverified, and they are kept visible rather than resolved.

## Licensure follows the patient, and Florida is the easiest first market

Across the states checked, the license requirement attaches to where the patient is located, not where the physician sits. Florida says so explicitly: telehealth care is deemed to occur where the patient is, and suit may be brought in the patient's county or Leon County ([Fla. Stat. 456.47](http://www.leg.state.fl.us/statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&URL=0400-0499/0456/Sections/0456.47.html), read as a summary). The same statute defines telehealth as using "synchronous or asynchronous" technology, excludes e-mail and fax, and says no prior history review or physical exam is required if the remote evaluation is sufficient to diagnose and treat. The standard of care is the in-person Florida standard. That matters for design: a structured platform case packet and a documented physician review fit the statute, while a loose email or text exchange may fall outside the "telehealth" definition. California and New York also recognize store-and-forward by statute, but Washington reportedly bars forming a relationship through email, instant messaging, text or fax alone, and that Washington bill's enactment is unconfirmed ([WA House Bill Report](https://lawfilesext.leg.wa.gov/biennium/2023-24/Htm/Bill%20Reports/House/5481-S.E%20HBR%20HCW%2024.htm)). No source found addresses AI-prepared intake specifically, so the team must treat the physician as fully responsible for the standard of care.

Physicians who are not Florida-licensed have three doors. A telehealth provider includes practitioners licensed under a multistate compact Florida belongs to, and out-of-state providers who register under 456.47(4). Registration requires an active unencumbered out-of-state license, no discipline in the prior five years, a Florida registered agent and malpractice coverage, and it excludes foreign licenses ([Fla. Stat. 456.47 (2025)](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2025/456.47)). The Interstate Medical Licensure Compact (IMLC) speeds multistate licensing but does not replace it: the physician still gets a separate license in each state. Florida passed SB 7016 in 2024 and became the 40th state ([Florida Board of Medicine](https://flboardofmedicine.gov/licensure-compact/)). The AMA reports **44 states plus DC and Guam**, over 200,000 licenses issued, a $700 application fee plus state fees, and a Letter of Qualification averaging about 38 days ([AMA](https://www.ama-assn.org/practice-management/digital-health/cross-state-licensing-process-now-live-30-states)). CCHP's Fall 2025 summary says **42 states**, DC and Guam ([CCHP](https://www.cchpca.org/2025/10/2025_ExecutiveSummary_final.pdf)). The notes could not load the IMLCC map, so the member count is a flagged conflict (plausibly just different dates) and the status of California, New York, Texas, Washington and Illinois must be checked on imlcc.org. Beyond the compact, CCHP reports that 38 states, DC and PR allow some exception to in-state licensure, usually consultation with an in-state provider who keeps ultimate responsibility, and 18 states offer telehealth-specific licenses or registrations; the consultation exceptions likely do not fit Checkpoint because the consumer, not a local clinician, initiates the request.

Unlicensed practice is the sharpest penalty exposure. In Florida it is a third-degree felony (a minimum $1,000 fine and one year of mandatory incarceration per the page summary), a second-degree felony if serious bodily injury results, and a cease and desist can issue to anyone who aids and abets by employing an unlicensed person, with $500 to $5,000 administrative citations and civil penalties per offense ([Fla. Stat. 456.065](https://florida.public.law/statutes/fla._stat._456.065), mirror site). The inference the notes draw is that the platform and the practice must never route a patient to a physician unlicensed in the patient's state, so license-state matching should be a hard gate in the MCP workflow rather than a policy.

Three scope decisions shrink the legal surface. First, controlled substances: the Ryan Haight Act requires an in-person evaluation before telemedicine prescribing of controlled substances, with narrow exceptions, and DEA's COVID-era flexibilities run through **December 31, 2026** under a fourth extension ([Hooper Lundy](https://www.hooperlundy.com/dea-issues-fourth-extension-of-telemedicine-flexibilities-for-prescribing-controlled-medications-through-2026/)). A special-registration final rule went to OIRA on August 25, 2026, and its text is unpublished ([Frier Levitt](https://www.frierlevitt.com/?p=60906)). Excluding all controlled substances avoids DEA registration and that year-end cliff entirely. Florida separately limits Schedule II telehealth prescribing to narrow settings and bars telehealth abortions (Fla. Stat. 456.47). Non-controlled e-prescribing rules were not researched. Second, consent: CCHP counts 45 states, DC and PR with some telehealth consent requirement, and California requires verbal or written consent documented in the record ([Cal. B&P 2290.5](https://law.justia.com/codes/california/2020/code-bpc/division-2/chapter-5/article-12/section-2290-5/), 2020 version). Consent in the MCP flow should state that review is asynchronous, that there is no live exam, that it is not for emergencies, which state license the physician holds, and how data is handled. Third, emergencies: no statute found imposes a specific emergency-referral duty on asynchronous telehealth, so red-flag triage (chest pain, breathing difficulty, stroke signs) must be built from the standard of care and displayed immediately with 911 instructions, before any queue.

## Physician-owned practice, flat fees and clean referral economics

CPOM bars lay corporations from practicing medicine or employing physicians to protect independent judgment. More than 30 states have some version ([McDermott](https://www.mcdermottlaw.com/insights/under-new-management-not-so-fast-federal-cpom-bill-targets-mso-structures-friendly-physician-model/)). Florida has no specific statute barring physicians from practicing through a corporation, and the Board of Medicine has said the unlicensed-practice prohibition does not bar physicians employed by a Florida corporation, but that comes from a 2013 article and no 2025 or 2026 Florida confirmation was found ([Epstein Becker Green](https://www.ebglaw.com/health-law-advisor/corporate-practice-of-medicine-the-unseen-hurdle-in-telehealth)). One commentator ranks New York, Washington and Texas as strictest, with California relatively strict ([Harris Sliwoski](https://harris-sliwoski.com/cannalawblog/ketamine-ventures-the-strictest-corporate-practice-of-medicine-states/)); that is a law firm blog view, not a legal classification, and it does not discuss Illinois or Florida. Illinois retains the doctrine for non-hospital corporations after *Berlin v. Sarah Bush Lincoln Health Center* ([Ill. S. Ct.](https://law.justia.com/cases/illinois/supreme-court/1997/81059.html)). The critical point is that Florida's permissiveness does not help patients elsewhere, because CPOM follows the patient's state, so a Florida-only entity does not solve a multistate launch. Design to the strictest state served, or geofence strict states at launch.

The standard solution is an MSO and a "friendly" PC: a physician-owned professional entity employs or contracts the clinicians and holds the patient relationship, while the technology company provides administration, technology and billing under a management services agreement. Every peer follows the pattern. Teladoc's 2015 agreement uses fixed monthly fees, says the PA retains control of medical decisions and that Teladoc will not influence physician judgment, and keeps physicians on the PA's payroll ([Justia](https://contracts.justia.com/companies/teladoc-health-inc-3746/contract/441974)); K Health says it "is not a provider of clinical advice" and works through affiliated professional corporations ([K Health Terms](https://khealth.com/terms-of-use)); Included Health works through physician-owned medical groups ([Included Health ToS](https://includedhealth.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Included-Health-Terms-of-Service-_032526-2.pdf)); Amazon's SteadyMD agreement names a Missouri professional corporation as the treating party ([Amazon/SteadyMD](https://clinic.amazon.com/legal/steadymd)). Scrutiny is rising. Oregon SB 951 limits MSO authority over hiring, compensation, pricing and billing, though telemedicine entities with no Oregon patient-care location get an exception ([Foley](https://www.foley.com/?p=113615)); California SB 351 and AB 1415 took effect January 1, 2026 and target private equity interference and MSO transactions ([Cooley](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2025/2025-12-16-californias-new-laws-what-private-equity-needs-to-know-about-healthcare-investment-restrictions)), though whether a venture-backed startup is covered turns on definitions. The federal Stop Corporate Takeovers of Physicians Act (H.R. 10444), introduced September 16, 2026, would create a first federal CPOM ban, bar MSO de facto control and require arm's-length fees, but it is introduced only and Simpson Thacher considers it unlikely to advance this Congress ([Simpson Thacher](https://www.stblaw.com/about-us/publications/view/2026/09/25/stop-corporate-takeovers-of-physicians-act-of-2026-bill-would-establish-a-federal-corporate-practice-of-medicine-ban-and-restrict-management-services-organization-arrangements)).

Money flow is where Florida law bites. The Medical Practice Act bars "split-fee arrangement in any form whatsoever" for patient referrals (Fla. Stat. 458.331(1)(i)), and historical Board declaratory statements conflict: a percentage of collections for space, equipment and billing was allowed in one (Lundy, 40%), while another (Bakarania) said a percentage of revenue from management services and practice enhancement is not permissible but a reasonable flat fee was acceptable ([Florida Bar Journal, 1998](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/fee-splitting-and-the-management-of-medical-practices-a-history-of-board-of-medicine-declaratory-s/)). That article is old, and current Board positions were not confirmed. The Patient Brokering Act (Fla. Stat. 817.505) criminalizes kickbacks for patient referrals regardless of payer, with third-degree felony exposure and fines of $50,000 to $500,000 depending on patient counts ([Fla. Stat. 817.505](https://florida.public.law/statutes/fla._stat._817.505)). Its "free consumer information service" exception requires fixed fair-market fees and no diagnostic services, so it likely does not protect a service that includes physician review (the notes' inference). The federal Anti-Kickback Statute reaches only federal health care program business, and a cash-pay consumer service is largely outside it, but that scope statement is background knowledge the notes could not source and needs confirmation. The most relevant precedent is OIG Advisory Opinion 25-03 (June 2025), which approved a PC/MSO arrangement with independently valued fair-market fees paid regardless of reimbursement ([JD Supra summary](https://production.jdsupra.com/legalnews/oig-issues-favorable-advisory-opinion-7170176)), limited to its facts and unverified against OIG's text. The practical design: the PC receives patient or operator payments, pays Checkpoint a flat or tiered fair-market monthly fee supported by a valuation, and pays physicians a flat per-review fee set by the PC. Avoid percentage splits, bank-account sweeps and payments to or from labs, pharmacies or downstream providers for referrals. Because Checkpoint "routes" cases, a per-case MSO fee could look volume-linked, so document its fair-market rationale. Physician classification is a separate risk: a February 2026 putative class action alleges misclassification of per-patient-paid telehealth physicians ([Reed Smith](https://www.reedsmith.com/articles/worker-misclassification-in-telehealth-new-california-class-action-signals-litigation-risk-for-contractor-arrangements/)), and the CPOM-friendly rule of no hours, shifts or throughput monitoring by the MSO also helps on classification.

## Consumer health data sits mostly outside HIPAA, and that is where enforcement lives

HIPAA status turns on whom Checkpoint acts for. A covered entity is a health plan, clearinghouse, or provider that transmits health information electronically in standard transactions such as insurance billing, so a cash-only practice may not be one. A business associate (BA) is a person who, on behalf of a covered entity, creates, receives, maintains or transmits protected health information (PHI) for a regulated function ([45 CFR 160.103](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/160.103)). The notes' working view, labeled inference and needing counsel, is a two-regime boundary: symptom text a consumer initiates through Claude is arguably consumer data held for the consumer, and once Checkpoint transmits packets to the practice and performs intake, routing, hosting and audit functions for it, Checkpoint is plausibly a BA of the practice. A BA agreement must cover permitted uses, safeguards, breach reporting, subcontractor flow-down, access and amendment rights, and return or destruction at termination ([45 CFR 164.504(e)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/164.504)). The conservative design is to sign a BAA with the practice and treat the practice-bound flow as PHI. For evaluation data, de-identification under 45 CFR 164.514 offers Safe Harbor (18 identifiers removed plus no actual knowledge) or Expert Determination ([eCFR 164.514](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-C/part-164/subpart-E/section-164.514)); free-text symptom narratives make Safe Harbor hard, so Expert Determination is the more defensible route, and a limited data set requires a data use agreement. Deletion promises must carve out the practice's medical record, since Florida requires physician records to be kept at least five years from last patient contact ([Fla. Admin. Code 64B8-10.002](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/florida/Fla-Admin-Code-Ann-R-64B8-10-002)), while HIPAA's six-year clock covers compliance documentation, not medical records.

The Anthropic layer is a trap for the unwary. A BAA covers only specific commercial products once activated: Claude Enterprise with HIPAA enabled and the first-party Messages API are covered, while Free, Pro, Max and Team plans cannot be HIPAA-enabled, and MCP connectors are "available but not covered" ([Anthropic: BAA for commercial customers](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/8114513-business-associate-agreements-baa-for-commercial-customers); [HIPAA-ready Claude Enterprise](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/13296973-hipaa-ready-claude-enterprise-offering)). So a consumer typing symptoms into consumer Claude is outside any BAA, and Checkpoint's connector cannot retroactively cover that chat. Consumer retention is 30 days by default and up to five years if the user allows training ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/updates-to-our-consumer-terms)). If Checkpoint uses the Claude API for redaction or summarization after handoff, it needs its own BAA, HIPAA-enabled organization and no excluded features such as Batch or Files, and the BAA's covered-model terms conflict with zero data retention ([Claude API docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/zero-data-retention)). The consent screen should tell users plainly that the chat provider is a separate party.

Where HIPAA does not apply, the FTC does. The amended Health Breach Notification Rule (final rule May 30, 2024) treats unauthorized disclosure, not only hacking, as a "breach of security" for a "vendor of personal health records"; notice is due within 60 days, with FTC notice at the same time for 500 or more individuals ([16 CFR 318.2](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/318.2); [16 CFR 318.4](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/318.4)). The enforcement record is concrete: GoodRx paid a $1.5 million civil penalty in its first HBNR case ([Arnold & Porter](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/blogs/enforcement-edge/2023/02/ftc-fines-goodrx)); BetterHelp paid $7.8 million in refunds and was barred from sharing health data for advertising ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/03/ftc-ban-betterhelp-revealing-consumers-data-including-sensitive-mental-health-information-facebook)); Premom received a $100,000 civil penalty judgment ([FTC statement](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/2023186easyhealthcarereasonforsettlement.pdf)), although a HIPAA Journal headline says "$200,000" (the notes attribute the difference to state AG settlements, unverified) ([HIPAA Journal](https://www.hipaajournal.com/200000-penalty-for-impermissible-sharing-of-premom-app-users-health-data/)); Cerebral's April 2024 order exceeded $7 million, with $5.1 million in partial refunds and a $10 million penalty suspended after a $2 million payment ([Healthcare Dive](https://www.healthcaredive.com/news/cerebral-federal-trade-commission-7-million-fine-data-sharing-privacy/713295/)). On July 29, 2026 the FTC, California and Utah sued Hims & Hers over pixels and customer-list uploads to ad platforms, inconsistent privacy statements and subscription practices; Hims denies it, and the allegations are unproven ([McDermott](https://www.mcdermottlaw.com/insights/ftc-two-states-take-aim-at-hims-hers-subscription-and-health-data-practices/)). The lesson for Checkpoint is design discipline: no third-party ad or analytics pixels on intake or consent pages, no customer-list uploads, and privacy statements that match actual flows. OCR's tracking-technology bulletin was partly vacated in June 2024 and HHS dropped its appeal, but that gives little comfort because the FTC and state wiretap suits reach the same conduct ([Proskauer](https://proskauer.com/blog/shortly-after-its-online-tracking-technologies-bulletin-is-declared-unlawful-hhs-ocr-stands-down-withdraws-appeal)).

State law adds consent mechanics. Washington's My Health My Data Act is the highest-risk statute: opt-in consent to collect, separate consent to share, signed authorization to sell, and a private right of action ([Fisher Phillips](https://www.fisherphillips.com/en/news-insights/beyond-hipaa-states-join-trend-to-protect-consumer-health-data.html)); the first class action was filed against Amazon in February 2025, with no outcome found ([WilmerHale](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/wilmerhale-privacy-and-cybersecurity-law/20250220-first-lawsuit-filed-under-washingtons-my-health-my-data-act)). Nevada and Connecticut have similar consent structures without private rights, and New York's health privacy bill was vetoed ([Healthcare Brew](https://www.healthcare-brew.com/stories/2026/01/12/new-york-governor-vetoes-health-data-privacy-bill)). Florida's exposure is mainly the Florida Information Protection Act, which counts medical history and diagnosis as personal information and requires notice within 30 days ([Fla. Stat. 501.171](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2024/501.171)); its Digital Bill of Rights applies only to very large companies ([DWT](https://www.dwt.com/gcp/state-privacy-laws-tracker/broad-state-privacy-law-tracker/florida)). California's SB 690 (signed September 30, 2026, effective January 1, 2027) ends private pen-register claims over website tracking but leaves the CIPA wiretap sections 631 and 632 untouched, so chat-transcript interception theories survive ([K&L Gates](https://www.klgates.com/thought-leadership/California-Narrows-CIPA-Litigation-SB-690-Ends-Pen-Register-and-Trap-and-Trace-Claims-10-8-2026)). California CMIA and CCPA, Texas HB 300 and TDPSA, and Florida's wiretap law (chapter 934) were not verified from primary sources. The HIPAA Security Rule overhaul remains a proposal as of July 2026 per [Physicians Practice](https://www.physicianspractice.com/view/hipaa-security-rule-postponed-privacy-rule-imminent), but building to it (MFA, encryption, audit logs) is cheap insurance. The consent screen should have unbundled toggles for collecting data, sending it to a named practice, optional de-identified evaluation use (default off) and optional marketing (default off), with no pre-checked boxes and versioned, hashed consent records.

## FDA, state AI laws and liability reward a physician in the loop, not a disclaimer

FDA's January 2026 clinical decision support (CDS) guidance exempts software that supports a health care professional and lets that professional independently review the basis for its output, but existing digital health policies "continue to apply to consumer or patient-facing CDS," and software that guides escalation for conditions needing immediate intervention remains under oversight ([Arnold & Porter](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/advisories/2026/01/fda-cuts-red-tape-on-clinical-decision-support-software); [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/en-pl/insights/publications/2026/01/fda-updates-its-clinical-decision-support-and-general-wellness-guidances-key-points)). Checkpoint's AI draft goes to a physician, which fits the HCP-facing criteria, but the pre-review patient-facing pieces (triage and red-flag messaging) are the exposed part. The notes' inference, untested in any authority, is that the strongest position is that the physician, not software, delivers the clinical advice. Keep pre-review content to general education and generic emergency messaging, document intended use, and do not claim an FDA exemption. FDA looks at intended use including marketing and statements, a "talk to your doctor" disclaimer may not change device status, and enforcement against chatbots has so far come mainly from states, not FDA ([Cooley](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-06-25-ai-chatbots-medical-claims-draw-regulatory-scrutiny); [Hall Render](https://hallrender.com/2026/08/26/fda-seeks-input-on-the-future-of-genai-enabled-medical-device-oversight/)). The two law firm summaries disagree on how the 2026 guidance treats automation bias, so read the guidance itself.

State AI-in-healthcare laws converge on three themes: do not imply the AI is licensed, disclose AI use with a path to a human, and require licensed review. California AB 3030 (effective January 1, 2025) requires an AI disclaimer and human-contact instructions for generative AI clinical communications, and AB 489 bars AI terminology, interfaces or post-nominals implying licensed oversight ([Fenwick](https://www.fenwick.com/insights/publications/the-new-regulatory-reality-for-ai-in-healthcare-how-certain-states-are-reshaping-compliance)). Texas SB 1188 (effective September 1, 2025) requires practitioners to review AI recommendations and remain responsible, and TRAIGA (effective January 1, 2026) requires disclosure of AI use in diagnosis or treatment ([Fenwick](https://www.fenwick.com/insights/publications/the-new-regulatory-reality-for-ai-in-healthcare-how-certain-states-are-reshaping-compliance)); JD Supra reports TRAIGA penalties of $10,000 to $200,000 per violation ([JD Supra](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/new-year-new-ai-rules-healthcare-ai-9758831/)), while Manatt frames TRAIGA mainly as a sandbox law, so health-specificity is a framing difference. Illinois HB 1806 and Nevada AB 406 bar AI from therapy and mental-health care, and Delaware HB 191 reportedly bars non-human entities from practicing medicine or using titles like "doctor" ([Cooley](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-06-25-ai-chatbots-medical-claims-draw-regulatory-scrutiny)). Florida has no found health-AI statute, but its Attorney General sued OpenAI in state court and a chatbot bill (HB 659) was pending as of January 2026 with final status unknown ([WDBO/AP](https://www.wdbo.com/news/business/florida-sues-openai/ABJFAGFG7E5SRFGVA43V4KZ6UQ/)). A December 11, 2025 executive order seeks to preempt state AI laws, but the notes found no evidence it invalidated any health-AI law ([Manatt](https://www.manatt.com/insights/newsletters/client-alert/trump-executive-order-targets-state-ai-laws-raising-questions-and-risks)). The "holding out" risk is real: on May 1, 2026 Pennsylvania's medical board sued Character Technologies after a chatbot persona claimed a psychiatry license and gave a fabricated license number ([Cooley](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-06-25-ai-chatbots-medical-claims-draw-regulatory-scrutiny)). Cooley recommends enforcing limits through model constraints, not disclaimers. Marketing should say "physician-reviewed guidance," never "AI doctor."

On liability, the law is unsettled but points at the signing physician first. The Doctors Company says malpractice law "provides no clear allocation of liability" among physicians, health systems and tech companies, with physicians carrying primary risk because they sign the record ([Physicians Practice](https://www.physicianspractice.com/view/tech-at-warp-speed-medical-practice-liability)). Chatbot companies face design-defect and duty-of-care theories that often sidestep Section 230 (the court in *Garcia v. Character Technologies* let claims proceed, and Character.AI and Google agreed to settle in January 2026, leaving no appellate ruling) ([Quinn Emanuel](https://www.quinnemanuel.com/the-firm/publications/artificial-intelligence-update-april-2026/); [Insurance Journal](https://www.insurancejournal.com/news/national/2025/05/23/824758.htm)). Health-specific suits against OpenAI are pending: *Turner-Scott* (May 2026, alleged dosing advice before a fatal overdose) and *Wan* (October 2026, alleged advice to taper an anti-seizure drug to zero) ([Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/tech-and-telecom-law/openai-hit-with-overdose-suit-centered-on-chatgpt-medical-advice); [HLTH](https://hlth.com/insights/news/openai-faces-wrongful-death-lawsuit-over-alleged-chatgpt-medication-advice)). Both pleaded failure modes (unsupervised AI advising on dosing and discontinuation) are what a physician-first design plus a hard refusal on medication changes directly answers. Disclaimers helped OpenAI against defamation in *Walters v. OpenAI* ([Quinn Emanuel](https://www.quinnemanuel.com/the-firm/publications/artificial-intelligence-update-april-2026/)), but no source found shows a terms-of-service waiver defeating a wrongful-death claim. Ostensible agency is the platform's other exposure: if patients reasonably believe the doctors are the platform's own, vicarious liability follows, and ProAssurance lists mitigations (no logos on contractors, their own insurance, conspicuous non-employee notices) ([ProAssurance](https://riskmanagement.proassurance.com/hubfs/M6240_July2025ClaimsRxD4.pdf?hsLang=en)). Checkpoint appears inside Claude, so it is a double-brand situation and the practice's name must be prominent. Insurance is tightening: ISO generative-AI endorsements for CGL took effect January 1, 2026 and some E&O carriers exclude or sub-limit AI claims ([Olshan](https://www.olshanlaw.com/assets/htmldocuments/Legal%20Intelligencer%20-%20The%20Continued%20Rise%20of%20Generative%20AI%20Exclusions%20in%20Commercial%20Insurance%20Policies%20-%20What%20Policyholders%20Need%20to%20Know%20Crawford%205-15-26.pdf)), though no source verified exclusions in medical professional liability policies and no reliable premium figures were found, so the team should not quote costs. Automation bias is the signature plaintiff theory against the packet model. Utah's Doctronic agreement offers a template: a physician approves every AI renewal in phase one, disagreements get risk assessment, later phases sample, and monthly reports go to the state ([Utah agency reply](https://commerce.utah.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Medical-Board-Doctronic-Response.pdf)). Eleven of 14 Utah Medical Board members asked for suspension anyway, and a sandbox waives regulator enforcement, not private lawsuits (the notes' inference). For evidence, K Health and Cedars-Sinai reported physicians matched the AI about two-thirds of the time across 461 visits, in a vendor-affiliated study rated by experts rather than outcomes ([EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1078883)), and HealthBench measures single-response quality, not workflow safety ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.08775)).

## Foreign doctors are a later phase with no clean US legal hook

No state route found lets a foreign-only physician independently advise a US patient. Florida's out-of-state telehealth registration requires a license from another state, DC or a US possession, and does not mention foreign countries ([Fla. Stat. 456.47 (2025)](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2025/456.47)). The only hook is Fla. Stat. 458.303(1)(b), which exempts a physician "lawfully licensed in another state or territory or foreign country" when "meeting duly licensed physicians of this state in consultation" ([florida.public.law](https://florida.public.law/statutes/fla._stat._458.303)). The text has no telehealth language and does not define "meeting," and no authority was found applying it to asynchronous commercial consults. A 2023 vendor white paper says 49 of 54 jurisdictions have some out-of-state specialist path, but it concerns US specialists, not foreign-licensed ones ([Purview](https://www.purview.net/2023-licensure-rso)). Teleradiology is the practical precedent: overseas radiologists typically give preliminary reads while a US radiologist issues the final report, and malpractice coverage must span both sites ([AuntMinnie](https://cdn.auntminnie.com/industry-news/article/15564284/legal-ground-rules-guide-international-teleradiology-practice)). The defensible structure is a documented consult to a US-licensed doctor of record who independently reviews, adopts and signs, with no direct foreign-doctor communication to the patient.

India and Brazil each add local-law gaps. India's Telemedicine Practice Guidelines "do not cover consultations involving doctors or patients outside India," so there is neither authorization nor prohibition ([Khaitan & Co](https://khaitanco.com/thought-leaderships/Telemedicine-Practice-Guidelines)). India's DPDP Rules were notified November 13, 2025, with cross-border provisions in the final 18-month stage reported as May 12 or 13, 2027, and an April 2026 report says MeitY proposed shortening the timeline (final stage moved to November 13, 2026) with immediate cross-border limits for significant data fiduciaries, without saying whether that was notified ([AZB](https://www.azbpartners.com/bank/indias-digital-personal-data-protection-act-phased-rollout-and-key-compliance-milestones/); [Legal 500](https://www.legal500.com/intelligence/india/privacy/meity-plans-to-cut-short-dpdp-compliance-timeline-and-notify-cross-border-restrictions-for-sdfs)). That is a live conflict, and with today's date of November 7, 2026, the proposed accelerated date is days away. Brazil's CFM Resolution 2.314/2022 permits telemedicine "dentro do território nacional" and requires providers to be based in Brazil, with no provision on foreign patients, so a Brazilian CRM doctor treating US patients sits in a regulatory gray zone ([CFM 2.314/2022](https://sistemas.cfm.org.br/normas/arquivos/resolucoes/BR/2022/2314_2022.pdf)). CFM Resolution 2.454/2026, in force since August 10, 2026, keeps physicians fully responsible for AI output, requires disclosure to patients and bars delegating communication of prognosis or therapeutic decisions to AI ([Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/pt/insights/publications/2026/03/brazilian-cfm-issues-resolution-on-the-use-of-artificial-intelligence-in-medicine)). Brazilian LGPD transfers require standard contractual clauses (grace period ended August 23, 2025) and incident reporting within three days ([Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/pt/insights/publications/2025/08/end-of-grace-period-implementation-of-brazils-standard-contractual-clauses-in-international-transfers-of-personal-data)). Other US constraints: Medicare does not pay for services furnished outside the US ([42 CFR 411.9](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/chapter-IV/subchapter-B/part-411/subpart-A/section-411.9)), and Medicare Advantage plans collect offshore subcontractor attestations ([Wellcare form](https://www.wellcare.com/-/media/pdfs/california/provider/2025/ca_care_prov_offshore_subcontracting_attestation_form_2025_r.ashx)), so a payer or health-system customer will likely impose restrictions. HIPAA has no offshore prohibition, but HHS says location raises risk that must be analyzed, and foreign physicians accessing PHI would need subcontractor BAAs ([HHS FAQ 2083](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/faq/2083/do-the-hipaa-rules-allow-a-covered-entity-or-business-associate-to-use-a-csp-that-stores-ephi-on-servers-outside-of-the-united-states/index.html)). Translation matters too: under 45 CFR 92.201(c)(3), when machine translation is used and accuracy is essential or the text is technical, a qualified human translator must review it ([eCFR](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-A/part-92/subpart-C/section-92.201)), though whether Section 1557 applies to Checkpoint (it depends on federal financial assistance) was not established. The notes' phased path is the sensible reading: Phase 1 is US-licensed doctors in the patient's state; Phase 2 is US-licensed doctors physically abroad plus non-clinical foreign support (translation checks, packet prep) under subcontractor BAAs; Phase 3 is foreign-licensed consultants to a US doctor of record, only after state-by-state and local counsel sign-off. Judgment enforceability against Indian or Brazilian physicians was not researched, so keep the US doctor the defendant of first resort.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question from "is it legal?" to "which version is legal?" The version that works is narrow and boring on purpose: cash-pay or operator-pay, Florida-first, patient-state-matched licensed physicians in a physician-owned practice, flat fair-market MSO fees, no controlled substances, no medication changes, no mental-health output, and no tracking pixels. Those constraints are not drag on the product; they are what answer the pleaded failure modes in the 2026 chatbot suits and the enforcement theories in the Hims, Cerebral and GoodRx matters. The biggest unforced error available is architectural confusion: assuming Anthropic's BAA covers the consumer chat or the connector (it does not), or letting marketing language ("AI doctor," "HIPAA compliant") run ahead of the structure.

The deeper implication is that regulatory risk concentrates in the seams between regimes: consumer data versus PHI, the platform versus the practice, the US doctor of record versus the foreign consultant. A team that can draw those seams on one slide, and say honestly which are unverified, will look more credible to judges than one claiming blanket compliance. The international vision should be pitched as a phased roadmap gated on local counsel, not a launch feature.

## What to build and say at the hackathon

Build the controls that make the legal story visible in a demo. A license-state gate should refuse to route a case unless a physician licensed (or compact-licensed) in the patient's state is available. A deterministic red-flag screen should show 911 or 988 guidance immediately, before any queue, using static text rather than model output. An out-of-scope refusal should block controlled substances, medication starting, stopping or dosing changes, abortion-related care, and mental-health clinical output, with a handoff to in-person care. A consent screen should carry unbundled toggles (collect, send to a named practice, optional de-identified evaluation use, optional marketing), an 18+ attestation, a plain statement that the chat provider is a separate party, and a note that revocation cannot erase the practice's medical record. The physician view should label the packet as an AI-generated draft and let the physician accept, edit, reject, ask for more information or escalate, with no quotas or timers, and with an audit log of review time, edits and disagreements. Show the physician's real name, license state and practice name on every reply, label the AI as AI, and keep pseudonymized packets with the key held separately. Include a mock Utah-style phase plan (physician approves all, then sampling) and a metrics panel for red-flag escalation recall, physician-AI agreement, override rate and no-recommendation rate.

Say precisely, and no more. Describe Checkpoint as "physician-reviewed guidance routed through an AI assistant," and say a licensed physician in your state makes every clinical judgment. Describe the structure as a physician-owned practice with Checkpoint as administrative and technology services provider. Say the launch is US-only and Florida-first, with international doctors as a gated later phase. Say what you do not have: no clinical validation yet, no insurance quote, no counsel opinion, and no regulatory approval (if you mention a sandbox, say you would apply to Utah's Office of AI Policy and model the application on Texas's structure, and do not claim approval). Do not say "HIPAA compliant," "FDA exempt," "AI doctor," or "no liability because of our terms of service." Do not claim that Anthropic's BAA covers your flow.

## Likely judge questions and honest answers

1. **Isn't this the unlicensed practice of medicine, or an AI doctor?** No clinical judgment is made by software. The AI prepares a packet; a licensed physician in the patient's state reviews it and owns the advice, which matches Texas SB 1188's review requirement. We avoid titles like "AI Doctor," which California AB 489 targets. The risk is real if marketing or the interface implies a license, as the Pennsylvania suit against Character Technologies shows.

2. **Who is liable if someone is harmed?** Honestly, it is unsettled. The signing physician and their practice carry primary risk today, and the platform is exposed through negligence and ostensible agency. We reduce this with documented independent review, consent, conspicuous practice branding and insurance, but we cannot eliminate it.

3. **Won't doctors rubber-stamp the AI?** It is a real risk; commentators call clinicians "liability sinks." Our mitigation is design: no quotas or timers, logged disagreements, seeded-error audits, sampling, and a phased rollout modeled on Utah's Doctronic agreement. We have not yet measured the effect.

4. **Can Section 230 protect you?** We do not rely on it. AI output and physician advice are first-party content, and design-defect and duty-of-care claims against Character.AI survived early motions before settlement. No medical-AI Section 230 ruling was found, so this is inference.

5. **OpenAI is being sued over health advice. Why is Checkpoint different?** The suits (Turner-Scott, Wan) allege unsupervised dosing and medication-discontinuation advice. We refuse medication changes, never prescribe, and put a physician before any clinical advice. Those cases are unresolved, so our risk is lower, not zero.

6. **Can your terms of service waive liability?** Do not count on it. Disclaimers helped OpenAI on defamation in Walters, but we found no ruling letting a terms-of-service waiver defeat a wrongful-death or injury claim, and CA, TX and other states impose their own disclosure duties regardless.

7. **Is the doctor your employee? What about corporate practice of medicine?** No. Doctors work through a physician-owned practice, and Checkpoint is an administrative MSO on a flat fair-market fee, the structure Teladoc, K Health, Included Health and SteadyMD use. Florida is viewed as permissive, but that rests on a 2013 source, and strict states such as NY, TX, CA, OR and WA would need geofencing or specific structures. A federal CPOM bill (H.R. 10444) is introduced only.

8. **If you brand the doctors, aren't you on the hook for them?** Possibly, under ostensible agency. We show the practice's name and the physician's own license, disclose that the practice is independent, and require each practice to carry its own malpractice coverage. The more control the platform exerts over clinical workflow, the weaker this defense, so we deliberately do not control it.

9. **What happens in an emergency?** Red-flag symptoms trigger an immediate, static 911 message before any queue. Asynchronous review is not for emergencies and consent says so. No statute found imposes a specific escalation duty, so this rests on standard of care, and our escalation recall is unvalidated. Good Samaritan statutes would not shield a paid, scheduled service.

10. **Do you need FDA clearance?** We do not know yet, and we do not claim exemption. The January 2026 CDS guidance exempts HCP-facing software that allows independent review, which fits the physician-facing packet, but it keeps consumer-facing CDS under existing policy, and triage or escalation guidance for urgent conditions stays under oversight. We keep patient-facing pre-review content generic and want regulatory counsel to confirm.

11. **Is this HIPAA compliant?** We do not say that. The consumer-to-Checkpoint leg is arguably outside HIPAA and under the FTC and state law; once data goes to the practice, Checkpoint is plausibly a business associate and would sign a BAA. Anthropic's BAA does not cover consumer chats or MCP connectors, so we treat everything arriving through the connector as consumer health data until handoff. Counsel must confirm this split.

12. **How do you protect privacy, and what do regulators actually go after?** Consent with unbundled toggles, pseudonymization, an audit trail, no ad pixels, no customer-list uploads, and BAAs with vendors in the PHI chain. Enforcement in this sector has been privacy and subscription practices: GoodRx ($1.5 million), BetterHelp ($7.8 million), Cerebral (over $7 million), and the unproven July 2026 Hims & Hers suit. We also design to Washington's strict health data law.

13. **Doctors must be licensed where the patient is. How do you handle 50 states?** We start in Florida, where asynchronous telehealth is expressly allowed, then add states through IMLC-eligible physicians, since the compact still issues a license per state. A hard gate blocks routing to anyone not licensed in the patient's state. This limits supply early, and the compact's member count is disputed between sources (42 versus 44).

14. **Can you prescribe?** No. We exclude all controlled substances, which avoids DEA registration and the December 31, 2026 expiry of the telemedicine flexibilities, and we exclude medication changes and abortion-related care. Non-controlled e-prescribing rules were not researched, so we would need counsel before adding it.

15. **Why are you not paying referral fees or splitting revenue?** Florida bars split-fee arrangements and the Patient Brokering Act is an all-payer criminal statute. Our design is a flat fair-market MSO fee, a flat per-review physician fee set by the practice, and no payments to or from labs or pharmacies for referrals. Federal anti-kickback law likely does not reach a cash-pay service, but that is unverified, and a per-case fee could look volume-linked, so we document its rationale.

16. **Do state AI-in-healthcare laws apply?** Yes, and we design to the common denominator: clear AI labeling and disclosure with a human path (CA AB 3030, TX TRAIGA), no implied AI licensure (CA AB 489, Delaware), physician review of AI output (TX SB 1188), and no AI therapy output (IL, NV). Some effective dates and scope descriptions conflict between sources, and the federal preemption order has not been shown to displace them.

17. **What about international doctors in India and Brazil?** Not at launch. No state route found lets a foreign-only doctor advise a US patient; Florida's registration excludes foreign licenses, and the consult exemption in 458.303 is untested for asynchronous commercial use. India's telemedicine guidelines are silent on foreign patients, Brazil's CFM 2.314 limits telemedicine to Brazilian territory, and both have data-transfer rules (India's DPDP timing is disputed between sources). Phase 2 is non-clinical support and US-licensed doctors abroad; Phase 3 is documented consultants to a US doctor of record, with local counsel sign-off. Medicare does not pay for services outside the US, which affects payer deals.

18. **Is the AI's translation safe for Portuguese or Spanish speakers?** Not on its own. Under 45 CFR 92.201, machine translation of critical text must be reviewed by a qualified human translator if Section 1557 applies, and we have not established that it does. We would add human review of translated advice and say so.

19. **What clinical evidence do you have, and is HealthBench enough?** None of our own yet. The nearest analog is K Health with Cedars-Sinai (461 visits, about two-thirds physician-AI agreement, vendor-affiliated, expert-rated, not outcome-based). HealthBench measures single-response quality with model grading, not workflow safety, so we would use it for model selection and run prospective physician-adjudicated testing, reporting escalation recall, agreement and override rates, and seeded-error catch rates.

20. **Will insurers cover this, and does the business work?** Maybe, with work. AI exclusions are spreading in CGL, E&O and D&O policies, so we would negotiate carve-backs and confirm telehealth and AI-assisted review coverage in each practice's malpractice policy. We have no reliable premium figures and no verified unit economics: physician review time, malpractice premiums and compliance cost are the drivers, and consumer subscriptions carry FTC risk. Treat both as open questions; also note that the sector has seen retrenchment (Amazon Clinic closure and 98point6 turmoil, both weakly sourced), while systems like Cedars-Sinai, Northwell and Penn are adopting AI intake with clinicians in the loop.

21. **Could a regulatory sandbox protect you?** Utah's Office of AI Policy has signed health relief agreements, including Doctronic, and Texas has a defined sandbox application; Delaware's is not operational. A sandbox waives regulator enforcement, not private lawsuits, and Utah's medical board opposed Doctronic. We would apply, not claim protection.
